# Referee report: bridge-crz-ems-travel

Verdict recorded: confirmed. Recommendation: change the verdict to unsettled.

The re-estimates are in `analyses/critique/referee_crz.py` (`python -m analyses.critique.referee_crz ems`). It builds the monthly series and the regressions with its own code from the aggregates in `data/derived/congestion/`, and makes two new aggregate queries to 76xm-jjuj, fetched at 08:52 on 13 November: incidents by month, weekday, hour and dispatch area for Manhattan areas M1 to M3, and citywide levels by year. I did not read `docs/congestion_tradeoffs.md` or any other write-up.

## (a) Does the verdict follow from the registered conditions?

It follows from the registration's own support condition, and it does not follow from the verdict rules the registration cites.

The support condition has two parts: the estimate lies within 1.96 combined standard errors of the composed prediction, and it is at least D = 4 further from a proportional fall of -5.4%. The estimate is -0.85% (-3.03 to +1.37), z_chain is 0.66 and D against the proportional alternative is 17.0. Both parts are met.

PRECEDENCE rule 8 defines discrimination against no effect, not against an alternative of the registrant's choosing: "The test discriminates when D = z_null^2 - z_chain^2 >= 4. When it does not, the verdict is unsettled, however well the endpoint matches." Here D against no effect is 0.14. The outcome's own stance record says `"discriminates": false` and gives a stance of 0.042. The registration says the verdict is "by PRECEDENCE rules 1, 2 and 8" and then substitutes a different D for the one rule 8 defines. Rule 7 also gives a bridge that meets its endpoint condition the verdict `bridged`, not `confirmed`. Under the rules as written the verdict is unsettled. The registrant was open about this: the plan says the test "CANNOT tell the chain from no effect" and that a no-effect world would pass with probability 0.84. Being open about it in the plan does not change what the dashboard will show, which is the word "confirmed" beside a statement that travel time "changed ... by about -1.68%".

The timestamps are in order. EMS rows for 2019 to 2024 were fetched between 07:56 and 08:01. The registration, the three EMS scripts and the design file were committed on `prereg/bridge-crz-ems-travel` at 08:05:20 and are identical to the files on `main`. EMS rows for 2025 were fetched at 08:06:35 and 08:07:54, and for 2026 at 08:08:51 and 08:09:34. The registration hash matches. The push time cannot be read from the repository. No measure chosen after the data decided the verdict.

## (b) Errors in code, units, joins, dates, geography or uncertainty

The deciding numbers reproduce exactly: log estimate -0.0086, standard error 0.0113, z_chain 0.66, z_null -0.76, z_prop 4.18. Overnight is -4.01% (-6.32 to -1.65) and the triple difference is +3.29% (+1.44 to +5.19), as recorded. I found no error in units, dates or the peak-hour definition.

The prediction is quoted two ways. The statement and `graph_effect` give -1.68%, which is the median of the composed draws. The notes give -1.8%, which is the mean of the log (-0.0178, or -1.77%), and the z-scores use the mean. One of the two should be used throughout.

Zone assignment. Assigning by ZIP or by dispatch area gives the same answer. Manhattan dispatch areas M1, M2 and M3 hold 362,095 valid incidents in 2023-24 against 366,532 for the ZIP list. With the zone defined as M1 to M3, peak is -0.79% (-3.10 to +1.59), overnight -4.40% (-6.53 to -2.22) and the triple difference +3.78% (+2.04 to +5.55). The ZIP list contains one error: ZIP 10044, Roosevelt Island, falls in the range 10041 to 10048. It is outside the toll zone and is served from Queens (1,625 valid incidents in 2024 carry borough Queens and dispatch area Q6). It is under 1 percent of zone incidents and the dispatch-area variant, which excludes it, shows it does not matter. Incidents with no ZIP go to the comparison area. Their share is 1.0 percent in every year to 2025 and 2.8 percent in 2026, which bears on the secondary full-period estimate and not on the primary one.

The citywide rise in travel time. Mean travel time over all valid incidents was 559 s in 2023, 607 s in 2024, 651 s in 2025 and 724 s in January to September 2026. I looked for a change of definition and did not find one. Travel time is present for every valid incident in every year. The share recorded as zero seconds is 1.1 to 1.3 percent throughout. The twelve-month change is between 6 and 10 percent in every half-year from 2023 to 2025, with no step. The rise comes with other signs of a system under growing load: mean dispatch time went from 162 s in 2023 to 319 s in 2025 and 606 s in 2026, the share of held incidents from 12 to 20 to 28 percent, and the share with travel over 30 minutes from 2.1 to 3.6 to 4.5 percent. Two consequences follow for this claim. First, the share of incidents flagged as having no valid response time, which the analysis drops, rose from 4.4 percent in 2023 to 6.3 percent in 2025 and 9.5 percent in 2026, so the sample is increasingly selected and the selection need not be the same in the zone and outside. Second, a difference of 1 percent is being read against a common rise of 7 to 8 percent a year, and it is only a toll effect if the zone and the rest of the city would otherwise have risen in exact proportion. They did not do so before the toll, as the next paragraph shows.

The standard error is too small for the claim made. I ran the registered estimator (8 months against the preceding 24) at every false start date from January 2023 to May 2024, all before the toll. The 17 estimates range from -0.2% to +4.9%, nearly all above zero, with a standard deviation of 0.016 on the log scale against the HAC standard error of 0.011. Over all 41 false starts from October 2021 the standard deviation is 0.035. The series has a drift that the calendar-month model does not absorb. Variants of the pre-period move the estimate accordingly: 2024 only, -1.15% (-3.25 to +1.00); 2022 to 2024, +0.09% (-1.95 to +2.17); 2023-24 with a linear trend, -3.13% (-7.47 to +1.42). With the linear trend, z against the proportional alternative is 1.03, so the one thing the registered condition claims to establish, that the fall was less than proportional, does not survive a trend term. Against Manhattan north the estimate is +1.83% and against downtown Brooklyn and Long Island City +4.06% (+1.89 to +6.27), as the outcome reports.

Link 2 is not stable. The registered exponent of 0.317 comes from October 2019 to December 2024, where most of the variation in the speed ratio is the pandemic (standard deviation of the log speed ratio 0.146 over the whole series, 0.061 without March 2020 to June 2021). Without those months the slope is -0.62 (se 0.10). For 2022-24 it is -0.49 and for 2023-24, the registered pre-period, it is -1.13 (se 0.40), which is a proportional response. The proportional alternative that the test is said to reject is therefore inside the range of the registrant's own link when it is estimated on recent data. A placebo outcome also fails: the overnight ratio of zone to outside travel time has nearly the same slope on the taxi speed ratio (-0.29, se 0.06) as the peak ratio. Overnight streets are not congested, so that slope cannot be a response to traffic speed. It shows that the monthly co-movement used for link 2 is mostly something common to all hours, such as the number of people in the zone, and not the mechanism the chain assumes. The exponent is truncated at zero in the composition, which makes a negative prediction certain (probability 0.99997); with a mean 2.5 standard deviations above zero the truncation changes the interval only from -0.32 to -0.38 at the upper end.

## (c) Is the comparison with the research fair?

No research enters. The graph holds nothing on the link and both links are city data, one of them estimated by the registrant on the same EMS series that supplies the endpoint (earlier months). Link 1 is the speed of taxis and for-hire vehicles over all hours, CBD against Greater NYC. The endpoint is ambulance travel under lights and sirens in peak hours, to incidents in the zone, by units that may start outside it. The elasticity is meant to carry that difference, and section (b) shows it does not identify it.

The proportional alternative is not a position anyone holds. Ambulances do not move at the speed of general traffic, and the registrant's own design run put the response at about a third before any post-toll data were seen. Rejecting it carries little information.

## (d) Alternative explanation not addressed

The overnight result is the main one, and the outcome reports it without drawing the conclusion. In overnight hours the toll is a quarter of the peak toll and streets are uncongested, so the toll predicts little or no change. The zone's relative travel time overnight fell by 4.0%, more than four times the peak estimate. This implies three things. Something other than peak-hour traffic speed lowered the zone's travel time relative to the rest of the city in 2025, at all hours, by more than the whole composed effect. The peak estimate of -0.9% therefore cannot be attributed to the toll, since the same or a larger change appears where the toll is nearly absent. And if overnight is taken as the control, which the registration proposed for removing common shocks, peak travel time in the zone rose by 3.3% relative to it, which is 4.0 combined standard errors from the composed prediction and 3.5 from zero. On the registered secondary control the chain is rejected, in the wrong direction.

I do not think the triple difference should be believed either. Its false-start values for 2023-24 run from -7.4% to -4.0%, and the overnight series alone gives +4.4% to +12.1%, so neither is stable before the toll. The correct reading is that the zone's relative travel time moves by several percent for reasons the design does not control, in both periods of the day, and that an effect of 1 to 2 percent cannot be found in it.

In unadjusted levels, peak travel time in the zone went from 587 s in 2023-24 to 627 s in January to August 2025, and outside from 620 s to 675 s. Overnight the zone went from 466 s to 484 s and the outside from 511 s to 561 s. The overnight difference comes mostly from the comparison area getting slower at night, by 9.6 percent.

Two candidates for the common change are in the data. The number of incidents in the zone fell by 3.0% relative to the rest of the city in both peak (-4.3 to -1.7) and overnight hours (-5.4 to -0.5), which would free units at all hours. And calls of different priority moved in opposite directions: life-threatening calls at peak +5.2% (+2.6 to +7.9), with a 2024 placebo of +6.0%, and lower-priority calls at peak -2.4% and overnight -5.9% (-8.5 to -3.1). The all-incident endpoint averages a rise for urgent calls with a fall for others. The closure of Mount Sinai Beth Israel is registered as an alternative and is not examined.

## (e) Could the test have failed?

Against the alternatives that matter, no. At the observed standard error the support condition is met by any estimate from -3.04% to +0.99%. I simulated the registered rule: it returns "confirmed" with probability 0.80 if the toll had no effect on ambulance travel time, 0.87 if the chain is exactly true, 0.50 if travel time rose by 1 percent, and 0.01 if it fell in proportion to speed. The test separates the chain from one implausible alternative and from nothing else.

At the endpoint, z against the prediction is 0.66 and z against no effect is -0.76, so D is 0.14. For the whole period to September 2026 they are 1.29 and -0.22.

## The lives figure

The outcome gives 0.3 lives a year (-0.7 to 2.0) from the measured change, and the composed prediction gives 0.7 (0.0 to 2.5). Quoting these as "0.3 to 0.7" would present two central values as if they were a range. I reproduced both from the registrant's inputs. The interval carries the sampling error of the endpoint, the spread of five per-minute odds ratios and a uniform baseline survival of 3 to 10 percent. It leaves out the choices that move the answer most:

- which measured change is used. The registered peak estimate gives +0.3 (-0.7 to +2.0). The triple difference gives -1.4 (-4.1 to -0.2). Total response time, which is what the survival studies measure, gives -0.3 (-4.6 to +2.9). A true effect of zero gives 0.0 (-1.4 to +1.3).
- which calls are used. Cardiac arrests are life-threatening calls, and for those the zone's relative peak travel time rose by 5.2 percent. The figure applies the all-incident estimate of -0.9 percent to them.
- attribution. The figure treats the peak difference as the toll's effect, which section (d) shows is not supported.
- the count. The 1,331 "attended arrests" are incidents with final call type ARREST and disposition 82 or 83, of which 43 percent are pronounced dead on scene. The odds ratios and the baseline apply to arrests that receive resuscitation, and the data do not say how many of these do.
- the odds ratios come from observational gradients over minutes of response time, and are applied to a change of 3 seconds.

In absolute terms mean response time to arrests in the zone at peak rose from 362 s in 2023 to 371 s in 2024, and ambulance travel time rose citywide by 7 to 8 percent a year. The toll's contribution in lives, in either direction, is not identified by these data. The figure should be removed from any summary, or stated as "between about -1.5 and +2, sign not determined, depending on the comparison used".

## Can the claim as stated and labelled mislead?

Yes. The statement says travel time "changed ... by the amount composed ... about -1.68%". The verdict "confirmed" beside it will be read as: ambulances in the zone got faster by about 2 percent because of the toll. The data do not show an improvement (the interval includes zero and a rise of 1.4 percent), the control hours moved further in the same direction, urgent calls got slower, and the registered rule would have confirmed a no-effect world four times in five.

Recommendation: change the verdict to unsettled, under rule 8 as written (D against no effect 0.14). The note should say that a fall in proportion to speed is excluded by the registered estimate but not when a trend term is added, that no improvement is shown, and that the overnight control contradicts the toll as the cause. The lives figure should not be reported as a result.
