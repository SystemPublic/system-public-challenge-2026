# Referee report: transfer-chicago-concordance-matrix

Verdict recorded: confirmed. The referee did not register this claim and did not read the registrant's write-ups (`docs/round5.md`, `docs/what_the_graph_adds.md`). The referee read the registration, the outcome, the three registered scripts, the derived tables and the earlier report on the New York matrix. The re-estimate is in `analyses/critique/referee_chicago_matrix.py`. It computes the correlations with its own code and uses a different seed (77042, 2,000 draws). It fetches the community-area boundaries from the Chicago Data Portal (dataset igwz-8jzy) into the system temporary folder, because the repository holds no Chicago geometry.

## (a) Does the verdict follow from the registered conditions?

The verdict follows from the letter of the registration. Fifteen qualifying pairs include PM2.5, ozone or traffic, and all fifteen pair an exposure with a health outcome. Twelve agree with the literature's sign (80%), which is at least 60%. The lower bootstrap limit is 0.533, above 0.370. New York agrees in 6 of the same 15 pairs. Under rule 1 this gives confirmed. My own bootstrap gives the same interval (0.533 to 0.867), so the condition does not depend on the seed, as rule 9 requires.

The registered conditions were applied with two departures in detail. The function `verdict()` in `matrix.py` checks the 60% threshold and the lower limit against 0.370, but not the third registered condition, that the share exceeds New York's share on the same pairs. That condition is met (12 against 6), so the omission changes nothing here. The threshold 0.370 is treated as a constant, although New York's 10 of 27 has its own sampling error. The registration wrote it that way.

No measure chosen after the data decides the verdict. The order of events is consistent with registration before the Chicago values were read. The three scripts were committed at 01:22:54 (0ba7f1a), and their blob hashes and SHA-256 sums match those written in the registration. The registration was committed at 01:23:02 (499547c). The first Chicago Health Atlas cache file has a file time of 01:23:08, the indicator table 01:23:58, the results 01:25:33, and the outcome is stamped 01:37:37. There is no `prereg/` branch for this claim, and the local record shows only that the commit was on `origin/main` by 01:57, so the time of the push cannot be confirmed.

The set of 75 common pairs was not chosen after the Chicago outcomes. It is produced by the registered rule: the pairs that qualify in `literature_signs.csv` (written on 12 November) among the indicators that the registered fetch rule keeps. All 75 Chicago pairs are computable in New York, so the restriction to common pairs removes nothing from the Chicago side. The registration predicted fifteen air and traffic pairs (six, five and four), and the data have exactly those. The four indicators dropped for coverage (distress, traffic deaths, hearing and vision difficulty) removed five pairs, none of them among the fifteen.

The New York side was fully seen before registration, including the 6 of 15 on the same pairs and the 0 of 6 for PM2.5. The threshold is therefore a known low value, and the comparison is exposed to regression toward the mean. Across the other 60 Chicago pairs the agreement rate is 43 of 60 (72%), and across New York's other 60 it is 44 of 60 (73%). A second city with nothing special about its pollution would be expected near 70% on the fifteen pairs, which also passes the registered condition.

The statement has two parts. The first is a comparison of counts, and the registered condition tests it. The second is the clause "as the residential-sorting explanation of the New York result predicts for a city whose polluted areas are poor". No registered condition tests that clause, and sections (b) and (d) give reasons to doubt that the data support it. The label confirmed can apply only to the count.

## (b) Errors in code, units, joins, dates, geography or uncertainty

The numbers reproduce. My code gives 75 qualifying pairs in Chicago, 72 with a positive literature sign, 55 of 75 agreeing in Chicago and 50 of 75 in New York. On the fifteen air and traffic pairs it gives 12 in Chicago and 6 in New York with the deciding correlation, and 10 and 6 with the raw correlation. By exposure, Chicago has 5 of 6 for PM2.5, 4 of 5 for ozone and 3 of 4 for traffic, and New York has 0 of 6, 5 of 5 and 1 of 4. The Spearman correlation of PM2.5 with poverty is +0.29 (0.05 to 0.51) in Chicago and +0.11 (-0.23 to 0.44) in New York. The correlation of PM2.5 with population density is -0.53 in Chicago and +0.71 in New York. I found no error in the partial correlation, the transforms or the join by community-area number.

The outcome's notes contain one miscount. They say three agreeing pairs have a correlation below 0.1 in size. There are four: asthma with PM2.5 (0.07), depression with traffic (0.07), asthma with traffic (0.01) and low birthweight with traffic (0.01). Among pairs with a deciding correlation of at least 0.1 in size, Chicago agrees in 8 of 11 and New York in 4 of 13.

The count of twelve is less firm than the interval suggests. The mean bootstrap share is 0.70, not 0.80, because the two traffic pairs at 0.01 are positive in only 54% of resamples. Three per cent of resamples fall below 60%. Leaving out one community area gives between 10 and 12 agreeing pairs. Five of the fifteen pairs have a sign that is stable in 95% of resamples (four with PM2.5 and low birthweight with ozone), and all five are positive. No pair is stably negative.

The difference between the cities comes from one exposure. Without the PM2.5 pairs, Chicago agrees in 7 of 9 and New York in 6 of 9. Two of Chicago's three agreeing traffic pairs are the correlations of 0.01. The result is therefore that the PM2.5 pairs changed from 0 of 6 to 5 of 6, and six pairs that share one exposure are close to one observation.

Against chance the count is weaker than 12 of 15 looks. A binomial test against one half gives a one-sided p of 0.018, but it assumes fifteen independent pairs. I shuffled the three exposures together across areas, which keeps their correlations with each other and the correlations among the outcomes. The null count has a mean of 7.5 and a standard deviation of 2.3, its 95% range is 3 to 12, and the probability of 12 or more is 0.04. The probability of a result as far from 7.5 in either direction is 0.08. A Fisher test of 12 of 15 against 6 of 15 gives a two-sided p of 0.06, and it also assumes independent pairs. This shuffle ignores spatial dependence, which makes the result weaker still.

Spatial dependence is strong. Moran's I on shared boundaries is 0.83 for PM2.5, 0.83 for ozone and 0.35 for traffic. A plane in the centroid coordinates explains 85% of the variance of PM2.5 across community areas and 58% of ozone. PM2.5 ranges from 8.63 to 9.33 micrograms per cubic metre (coefficient of variation 1.8%, against 10.7% in New York) and rises toward the southwest. Ozone ranges from 66.7 to 69.4 ppb (coefficient of variation 1.0%). The Chicago PM2.5 variable is close to a compass direction.

The effective number of areas is small. With the Clifford and Richardson correction applied to the poverty-partialled ranks, the effective sample size for the PM2.5 pairs is between 12 and 31 for five of the six (54 for asthma), and for the ozone pairs between 16 and 45. At those sample sizes none of the fifteen deciding correlations has p below 0.05. The smallest are 0.08 for obesity and for diabetes with PM2.5. For PM2.5 with poverty the effective sample size is about 10, and the p value of +0.29 is 0.43. The outcome scores prediction (b) as a hit because the bootstrap interval excludes zero. The bootstrap resamples areas as if they were independent, so that interval is too narrow. Prediction (b) should be scored partial.

I ran three variants the registrant did not run. In the first I added the centroid coordinates to poverty as controls, which asks whether PM2.5 predicts health beyond the regional gradient. Chicago then agrees in 9 of 15, and the PM2.5 pairs agree in 1 of 6. New York agrees in 3 of 15. In the second I replaced PM2.5 with a plane tilted toward each compass direction in steps of five degrees. In Chicago 38% of directions give at least 5 of 6 positive pairs, 38% give at most 1 of 6, and 25% give something between. Any smooth gradient gives a result near all or none, because the six outcomes share a geography. In the third I added a second social control to poverty. With foreign-born share, high-school completion or unemployment added, Chicago agrees in 9 of 15, which equals the 60% threshold exactly. With density added it agrees in 12. New York stays at 6 in all four.

## (c) Is the comparison with the research fair?

The comparison is fair only at the level of sign, as in New York. The registration lists the differences in measurement, and the outcome repeats them. Two of these differences matter for the count. The asthma indicator is survey prevalence in Chicago and emergency visits in New York, and it supplies two of the four agreeing pairs below 0.1. The PM2.5 measure is a modelled regional surface with a range of 0.7 micrograms per cubic metre. The literature's positive sign comes mostly from studies with exposure contrasts several times larger. A contrast of 0.7 would not be expected to produce correlations of 0.35 to 0.55 with obesity, diabetes and premature death through any causal path, so the Chicago correlations reflect where people live. That reading supports the registrant's general point about sorting, but it also means that agreement with the literature's sign in Chicago is no more informative about the literature than disagreement was in New York.

The comparison between the cities is not like for like either. New York's PM2.5 comes from a street-level survey and is highest in the dense core. Chicago's comes from a regional model and is highest in low-density areas in the southwest. The cities differ in the instrument and in the geography of pollution, and the design cannot separate the two.

## (d) Alternative explanation not addressed

The main alternative is the registration's own first one: a smooth surface ranks areas by position in the region. The outcome reports the range of PM2.5 and the density correlation, but it does not test this alternative. My variants test it, and they support it. The PM2.5 pairs agree because PM2.5 rises toward the south and west and health is worse there. Once position is partialled out, 1 of 6 agrees.

The sorting explanation says that agreement should follow the correlation between pollution and poverty. The deciding correlation partials poverty, and agreement is higher after partialling (12) than before (10), so measured poverty does not carry the result. The pollution and poverty correlations of the two cities (+0.29 and +0.11) have overlapping intervals, and neither differs from zero at the effective sample size. Racial segregation, which the registration names, is not addressed, because the indicator set has no measure of it. The South and West sides are both poorer and closer to the direction in which the modelled PM2.5 rises. With two cities the design has one degree of freedom for the relation between agreement and sorting.

## (e) Could the test have failed?

The test could have failed in principle. In practice it had a high chance of passing without the sorting explanation, for two reasons. A city at the general agreement rate of about 70% passes the condition. A smooth exposure surface gives nearly all or nearly none of its pairs the same sign, and about three directions in eight give the favourable result in Chicago.

With the bootstrap standard error of about 0.085, the observed share is z = 2.4 above the predicted 60% and z = 5.1 above New York's 0.370. The second figure is the source of the recorded stance (0.987, z = 5.05). It treats community areas as independent and the fifteen pairs as free to vary separately. The exposure-shuffle null puts the count 1.9 standard deviations above its mean, and the spatial results put the effective evidence at about one exposure in one direction. The stance should be reduced to match.

The registered condition is met under the registered seed and under others, so rule 1 gives confirmed and I do not recommend a change of label. The outcome should carry a `verdict_note` and corrections. The note should say that what is confirmed is the count: Chicago's air and traffic pairs agree more often than New York's did. It should say that the difference comes from the PM2.5 pairs, that Chicago's PM2.5 is 85% a southwest gradient with a range of 0.7 micrograms per cubic metre, and that with position partialled out 1 of 6 PM2.5 pairs agrees. It should say that the test does not show that sorting explains the New York result. The corrections are: four agreeing pairs below 0.1, not three; prediction (b) partial, not hit; the stance reduced. If the project reads the clause about residential sorting as part of what the label covers, the verdict is unsettled.

Recommendation: keep confirmed under rule 1 for the count only, with a verdict_note that the result is one smooth PM2.5 gradient and not a test of the sorting explanation, prediction (b) rescored as partial, the count of small correlations corrected to four, and the stance reduced.
