# What the graph adds to a city's open data

This note summarises the work of 12 and 13 November 2026 for the presentation on 14 November. It answers one question: what can a team do with the System Public Graph (a database of findings extracted from published research, called the graph below) and a city's open data together that it could not do with either alone? A claim is a prediction with stated conditions for support and refutation, committed to the repository (registered) before its outcome data were read. A claim is confirmed when its support condition is met, contested when its refutation condition is met and unsettled when neither is. Each result below names its claim. Referees who did not see the registrants' reasoning re-estimated the deciding number of every claim cited here, including the ten registered on 13 November, except the first heat claim, which an earlier round reviewed. The detailed write-ups are `graph_experiments.md`, `graph_discovery.md`, `round2.md`, `round5.md`, `heat_solutions.md` and `heat_extremes.md`.

## Seven uses, each with a result

### 1. The graph changes the shape of a hypothesis

The first heat claim in this project found that shootings rise 14.6% per 5 °C. The usual explanation is that more people are outdoors. The graph has one finding on heat and shootings and none on a mechanism. It does hold findings from exercise physiology and a Korean health survey that heat reduces walking. Those findings changed the prediction from "outdoor activity rises with heat" to "outdoor activity stops rising on hot days while shootings keep rising". The city data show this (`heat-mech-outdoors`, confirmed). Street-noise complaints and bicycle counts stop rising at about 27 °C, and shootings counted by calendar date rise another 12% by 33 °C. In Chicago a registered test found the same flattening (`transfer-chicago-hot-end`, unsettled): bicycle-share trips change by 0.95 (0.90 to 1.01) from 27 to 33 °C while gunshot injuries rise by 1.06 (1.01 to 1.11). The test's second condition, on the share of the mild-day slope that trips remove, was not met. People outdoors can account for part of the slope on mild days and for none of it on hot days. The cause of the hot-day excess is open, and the excess is smaller than these figures say. A third of New York's shootings occur between midnight and 06:00 and were counted on a date whose maximum came later; with shootings before 06:00 counted with the previous day, the rise from 27 to 33 °C is 1.05 (0.99 to 1.12) in New York and 1.04 (0.99 to 1.08) in Chicago. A registered test of the previous night's minimum temperature was unsettled in both cities, and a referee kept that verdict (`heat-night-minimum`). In its six-hour blocks the excess appears between midnight and 06:00, at about 1.11 per 5 °C in each city, but that block was singled out after the data were seen, humidity was not tested, and the New York association with that night's minimum falls to 1.07 (0.99 to 1.16) once the next day's maximum and rain are in the model. The graph's findings on bedroom temperature, sleep and aggression predict about 1.01 per 5 °C, which this test cannot detect.

Three tests registered on 13 November extend this. New York has few days above 35 °C, so one test looked for a downturn in Phoenix, Tucson, Dallas and Austin (`heat-extremes-downturn`, contested). Aggravated assaults there rise by 1.060 per 5 °C (1.046 to 1.073) from 20 to 35 °C and by 1.003 (0.985 to 1.022) above 35 °C. At 38 °C and above they are 1.035 times the rate at 32 to 35 °C (1.008 to 1.063). The rate stops rising at about 35 °C and does not fall. Neither of two explanations of the New York slope was supported. Districts with less household air conditioning have a steeper slope (`heat-ac-shootings`, confirmed: a difference of 0.043 log points, 0.007 to 0.080; 0.01 log points is about 1%), but the difference arises on days below about 20 °C, when air conditioners are off. Shootings on weekdays out of school are 1.105 times those on school days (0.997 to 1.224; `heat-school-out`, unsettled).

### 2. The graph shows two literatures that do not cite each other

Subway entries in the business district show a larger Monday and Friday deficit against midweek: 5.1% in 2019 and 13.8% now (`transit-weekday-pattern`, confirmed). The attribution to hybrid work was not tested. A search of the graph's study variables returned two bodies of research on a day moved from office to home. Commuting studies say less commuting gives better sleep and mood and less back pain. Telework studies say more work at home gives worse sleep and mood and more back pain. The two predict opposite signs for six of ten outcomes. The commuting studies include designs that follow the same people over time. Thirteen of the sixteen telework abstracts read are surveys from 2020 and 2021. A reader who searched for "hybrid work and health" would have found only the second body.

### 3. The graph says in advance which tests cannot succeed

The graph supplies effect sizes, and the project multiplies them along a chain to predict the size of the city effect before any city data are read. For the health effects of the re-timed week the predicted effects are about a tenth of a percent, and the city's health survey can detect only differences of a few percent, so no test was registered. The same check showed that a chain from temperature through asthma to school absence predicts a ratio of 1.003 to 1.007, which thirteen years of daily attendance cannot detect. This saves a team from running tests that would return a null by design and reading it as evidence.

### 4. The graph marks where research is absent, and the city can measure there

Taxi demand, restaurant trade, daytime burglary and ambulance calls have no research in the graph as consequences of commuting or working at home. The city's daily data gave first estimates for three of them. Daytime EMS incidents in the business district fell 4.0% (2.1 to 5.7) on Mondays and Fridays against 1.2% elsewhere (`retimed-ems-presence`, confirmed; without one Midtown district the interval includes zero). Taxi and for-hire pickups followed entries with a slope of 0.50 (0.13 to 0.81), and the second registered measure did not agree (`retimed-taxi-fhv-pickups`, unsettled). Daytime burglary did not fall (`retimed-daytime-burglary`, unsettled after review). Of the first 44 claims, the graph links both ends for 6 and has nothing usable for 20. Congestion pricing, building permits, electricity and for-hire vehicle pricing have no findings.

### 5. The graph can be asked every question at once

A concordance matrix compared 56 neighbourhood indicators pair by pair with the graph's signs (`graph-concordance-matrix`, confirmed on the registered rate only). Of 1,540 pairs, 109 have enough findings to give a sign, and the city's correlation agrees with the literature in 66% of them. A rule that predicts a positive sign for every pair reaches 64%, so most of the agreement is the clustering of disadvantage, and a referee recommended unsettled. The useful result is by domain. Two health outcomes agree in 34 of 41 pairs. Pairs of any exposure with an outcome agree in 29 of 56, which is chance. Pairs that include air pollution, traffic or noise agree in 10 of 27, below chance. In New York the most polluted neighbourhoods are dense, rich and young, which may be the reason, but that explanation has not been tested (see the next paragraph). The matrix also lists 948 pairs that the city measures and the graph holds no finding on. Household air conditioning and birth outcomes, and evictions and diabetes, are among them.

The same matrix was then run for Chicago (`transfer-chicago-concordance-matrix`, confirmed for the registered count only). On the 75 pairs that both cities measure, pairs of air pollution or traffic with a health outcome agree with the literature in 12 of 15 in Chicago and 6 of 15 in New York. The difference is PM2.5 alone: its pairs agree in 5 of 6 in Chicago and 0 of 6 in New York, and without them the counts are 7 of 9 and 6 of 9. A referee showed that Chicago's PM2.5 measure varies by 0.7 µg/m³ and that 85% of the variation is a gradient toward the southwest; with position in the city adjusted for, 1 of the 6 pairs agrees. The evidence shows that the sign of the correlation between PM2.5 and health across neighbourhoods reverses between the two cities and follows the geography of each city's pollution measure. It does not show why New York disagrees with the research, and the explanation by where residents live is untested. Four of the twelve agreeing pairs have correlations under 0.1.

A search for untested chains listed 4,405 pairs of exposure and outcome that the graph links only through a third concept. After reading, 2 of the 20 best-scored are real chains: air pollution to hypertension to enlargement of the heart, and air pollution to dementia to delirium. The search also led to one association with no finding in the graph. School absence in New York is 10% higher on school days at or below 0 °C (5 to 15), and 7% higher with an influenza control. The chain through asthma predicts under 1% and does not explain it (`graph-triad-cold-asthma-absence`, unsettled as a chain).

### 6. The city data audit the graph

Between a fifth and a third of the graph records checked against PubMed abstracts were wrong or had lost a qualifier that changes the reading. Percentages are stored as ratios, reference groups are named as exposures, and "81% reduced odds" is stored as an odds ratio of 0.81. In one experiment 27% of relationships that report findings returned none through the API. Half of the well-studied relationships appear split between positive and negative findings, and in the 15 most evenly split urban relationships 12 hold no real disagreement: "cognitive impairment" and "cognitive function" share a concept. The largest disagreement between the city and the graph in the matrix was a grounding error, in which findings about a satellite instrument named for ozone were filed under ozone. A city's data are an independent check on the graph, and the disagreements point to the records to repair.

### 7. The graph lists what an intervention is linked to, across outcomes

For each of 25 interventions, such as street trees, housing repair, traffic calming and air conditioning, the graph was asked which of eight outcome domains research links to it (`all_boats.md`). Of the 200 cells, 110 are empty and 46 hold a benefit verified against an abstract. Only housing repair and insulation has experimental evidence of benefit in several domains: four, from a cluster randomised trial and a controlled study. No verified finding links any of the 25 interventions to housing cost, which is the harm most often claimed for urban greening. Of the 114 records read, 25 (22%) were wrong or had lost a qualifier. One test on New York was registered (`all-boats-trees-rent`, unsettled). Rent rose by 0.0127 log points per 100 added street trees (0.0018 to 0.0235), but the tree measure is only partly a measure of planting, and the slope is not stable in size or sign across forms of the model.

## From checking statements to simulating policy

New York began charging a toll on vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th Street (the toll zone) on 5 January 2025. The work on it went from testing statements to comparing versions of the policy (`congestion_tradeoffs.md`, `policy_simulation.md`). The graph supplied one link, from ambulance response time to survival after cardiac arrest: five findings, read against their abstracts, give odds of survival 1.02 to 1.24 times higher for each minute less of response time.

A large rise in storefront vacancy is ruled out. Vacancy in the toll zone changed by −0.47 percentage points relative to the rest of the city (−1.08 to +0.17). The upper limit is below the registered threshold of +1.2 points, so a relative rise of that size is excluded and a smaller one is not (`crz-storefront-vacancy`, confirmed). Whether ambulances got faster is open. Their peak travel time in the zone changed by −0.9% relative to the rest of the city (−3.0 to +1.4), and the test could not tell the predicted −1.8% from no effect (`bridge-crz-ems-travel`, unsettled).

A simulator then composed the links to compare toll schedules that vary by weekday or hour. Its central link is the elasticity of traffic speed to vehicle entries, the percentage change in speed for a 1% change in entries. Daily data cannot estimate it, because entries vary by only about 3% from day to day. The elasticity fitted on 2025 did not predict the first half of 2026, which was not used in fitting (`sim-crz-holdout-2026`, contested: the 2026 slope is 0.29 of the fitted one, 0.19 to 0.38). The simulator therefore draws the elasticity from −0.23 (−0.69 to +0.23). Against the current schedule, a toll of $12 on every weekday changes revenue by +$118 million a year (+90 to +150) and entries by −2.3% (−3.4 to −1.2), and the fall in entries is an assumption of the model. For speed, ambulance travel time, survivors of cardiac arrest, retail activity and injury crashes, every interval includes zero under every schedule.

## What the graph did not do

It did not choose the variables to adjust for. About one in seven of the concepts two variables share is a plausible common cause, and the rest are outcomes and mediators. Reading them still named covariates the analysts had not used, and road area was the only one that changed a result. It did not supply usable concepts for commuting, public transport, cycling, eviction or vacant land, which exist only as variable names. It does not record which variable of a finding is the exposure, and that is why most candidate chains failed: the middle concept was a consequence of both ends, which links nothing.

## Three requests to System

1. Store the direction of each finding: which variable is the exposure, and whether the variable name is negated.
2. Make every finding counted in a relationship retrievable through the API, and report signs in the relationship record.
3. Ground urban exposures (commuting, transit, cycling, eviction, vacancy, congestion pricing) as concepts.

## Other results of the two days

Complaints of street flooding in coastal ZIP codes on dry days double for each 10 cm of tide above a threshold, in a held-out decade (`further-tidal-flooding-trend`, confirmed: 2.00, 1.64 to 2.38). Data centres use about 1.0 to 2.3% of Manhattan's benchmarked electricity against 4.4% nationally (`further-datacentre-share-manhattan-v2`, confirmed, with one condition passing narrowly). The heat and shootings slope transfers to Chicago (`transfer-chicago-heat-shootings`, confirmed: 1.131 against 1.146 per 5 °C). The weekday pattern is 1.8 times larger in Chicago's Loop (`transfer-chicago-weekday-pattern`, contested as a transfer). Three city reports were reproduced from open data: housing completions (on a later data release) and business-district travel speeds matched, and traffic deaths did not (229 in the open data against 205 published).

`explore.md` maps film shoots, vacant lots, new shops and galleries, and none of its numbers was registered. Tax lots recorded as vacant land fell from 43,633 in 2002 to 24,663 in the current lot table. In `affordability.md`, 8 of the 22 areas with the lowest rents in 2000 had rent and the share of college graduates rise faster than the city's (36%, 20% to 57%; `afford-gentrification-prevalence`, confirmed). The number of poor residents fell more in those eight than in the other fourteen (`afford-poor-count-shift`, confirmed).

## What the referees changed on 13 November

A referee rebuilt the deciding number of each of the ten claims registered that day with separate code, and all ten reproduced. One verdict changed, from confirmed to unsettled for `bridge-crz-ems-travel`, and the others are now read more narrowly. The table gives the eight changes not already stated above. Counting shootings and assaults before 06:00 with the previous day, as in section 1, altered three results.

| Claim | What changed in the reading |
|---|---|
| `bridge-crz-ems-travel` | Confirmed became unsettled. Whether lives were saved or lost is not determined. |
| `heat-ac-shootings` | With the day counted from 06:00 the difference is 0.025 (−0.011 to 0.061), and the support condition is no longer met. |
| `heat-extremes-downturn` | With the day counted from 06:00 the ratio at 38 °C and above is 1.030 (0.994 to 1.068). |
| `heat-school-out` | About half of the excess is holiday nights counted on the next weekday. With the day counted from 06:00 the ratio is 1.044 (0.951 to 1.146). |
| `heat-ac-heat-calls` (contested) | Protection by air conditioning at home is excluded only if more than about 17% of ambulance calls for heat illness come from homes. |
| `afford-gentrification-prevalence` | The registrant saw a distorted version of the outcome before registering. Low-rent areas cannot be told apart from the others (36% against 18%, p = 0.21). |
| `afford-poor-count-shift` | The condition is met by 0.02 log points (−0.283, −0.544 to −0.021). A fall in a count is not evidence of displacement. |
| `sim-crz-holdout-2026` | Two storm days carry 84% of the variation that fixes the result. |
