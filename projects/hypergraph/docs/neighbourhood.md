# Neighbourhood conditions: vacant land, noise complaints and rodents

This note examines three questions about neighbourhood conditions in New York City where the research evidence and the city's measures might disagree. Two were registered as confirmations (city data expected to agree with the research) and one as a conflict (city data expected to disagree). For each, the research evidence comes from the System Public Graph (API v1.0, Dataverse v2.1) and from abstracts read in PubMed. The city evidence comes from NYC Open Data, the Health Department's Environment and Health Data Portal (EHDP), the US Department of Transportation noise map, the New York State Liquor Authority and the American Community Survey (ACS). Each hypothesis, with its predicted direction and size, was committed to the repository before the city data that test it were queried. A claim is confirmed when the city estimate has the predicted sign and its 95% interval excludes zero, contested when it has the opposite sign and its interval excludes zero, and unsettled when its interval includes zero. For the conflict claim, the predicted sign is the sign of the registered contrast. Separately, each prediction is scored hit, partial or miss against the direction and size that were registered. The registrations are in `claims/registered/nbhd-*.json` and the outcomes, with every dataset version, in `claims/outcomes/nbhd-*.json`. The scripts in `analyses/neighbourhood/` rebuild every number here; `base.py` runs first.

## Vacant land and shootings (nbhd-vacant-shootings, confirmed)

The graph holds five relevant findings, all from older industrial cities. Branas, Rubin and Guo (2013, PMID 23814668) studied 1,816 Philadelphia block groups and found that aggravated assaults rose by a factor of 1.18 (1.12 to 1.25) for each step up in a categorised count of vacant properties. A Philadelphia case-control study found lower odds of adolescent homicide near maintained vacant lots (OR 0.17, 0.03 to 0.81; PMID 26954939). In a Philadelphia cluster randomised trial, remediating abandoned houses reduced gun assaults by 13% and shootings by 7%, which was not significant (PMID 36469329). In Rochester, New York, each demolition was followed on average by 14% fewer shootings nearby in the next year (PMID 34876475). The best-known study is not among the variables a search of the graph returns. In that trial (Branas et al. 2018), greening 541 Philadelphia lots reduced gun violence by 29% in neighbourhoods below the poverty line. It was published in PNAS (doi 10.1073/pnas.1718503115). The related mental-health trial was published in JAMA Network Open.

The registered prediction was that the association would transfer to NYC but more weakly, with an adjusted rate ratio of 1.00 to 1.10 per quintile. The registration gave two reasons: NYC has less vacant land than Philadelphia, and more of it is held for construction. Vacant land (MapPLUTO land-use code 11, version 26v2) makes up 7.8% of NYC tax-lot area. The test used 2,208 census tracts (2020 boundaries) with at least 500 residents and 6,913 shooting incidents from 2019 to 2024 in the NYPD table `5ucz-vwe8`. The model was a negative binomial regression with population as the offset and with poverty, population density and borough as covariates. The quintile entered as a linear score from 1 to 5, so the rate ratio is per quintile step.

The rate ratio was 1.17 (1.12 to 1.22) per quintile step. This is close to the Philadelphia estimate (1.18) and above the predicted range of 1.00 to 1.10. Adjusting for poverty did not reduce it (unadjusted 1.16). The rate ratio was 1.17 with Staten Island excluded and 1.13 with the non-Hispanic white share added as a covariate. Shootings rose from 0.96 per 10,000 residents per year in the lowest quintile to 1.67 in the highest. Within the tracts in the highest poverty tercile, the rate rose from 2.1 to 2.9 per 10,000 residents per year between the lowest and highest quintiles.

The association is not linear in vacant area. Per standard deviation of the untransformed share the rate ratio was 0.99 (0.94 to 1.05), because the top quintile runs from 2.8% to 94% of lot area and the tracts with the most vacant land are industrial or waterfront tracts with few residents. The rise in shooting rate occurs in the lower range of vacant-land share, where a tract has a few scattered vacant lots. An exploratory split, not registered, used the untransformed shares of private and City-owned vacant land in one model. The rate ratio was 1.13 per SD (1.03 to 1.22) for private vacant land and 0.96 (0.91 to 1.01) for City-owned vacant land.

The verdict is confirmed. The prediction was scored partial because the direction was right and the magnitude was wrong. Because the NYC result is ecological, it does not show that vacant lots cause shootings in New York. Showing that would need a lot-level design, such as shootings within 100 to 200 m of lots before and after they are built on, cleaned or greened, and a split of lots held for construction from lots vacant for three years or more.

The NYPD table has an error. In `5ucz-vwe8`, as fetched on 12 November 2026, the column named latitude holds longitudes and the column named longitude holds latitudes. The script detects this and swaps them. Incident counts per year agree with the archived table `833y-fsy8` to within one incident.

## 311 noise complaints as a measure of exposure (nbhd-noise311-exposure, unsettled)

The graph holds research on measured or modelled noise. Traffic noise is linked to arterial hypertension (12 findings, 8 studies), stroke (14 findings, 8 studies), diabetes (21 findings, 8 studies) and sleep (3 findings, 2 studies). One meta-analysis gives an odds ratio for hypertension of 1.11 (1.00 to 1.23) per 10 dB(A) (PMID 24952459). The graph holds no finding on noise complaints. Studies of New York, outside the graph, report that 311 complaints vary with reporting propensity: Legewie and Schaeffer (2016, American Journal of Sociology) found more neighbour complaints at blurred ethnoracial boundaries, and Kontokosta and Hong (2021) found that complaint behaviour differs by income, race and tenure.

The registered hypothesis was that, across census tracts, residential noise complaint rates are more strongly associated with neighbourhood change than with modelled noise. The registered test was the difference between the coefficient for rent change (ACS 2008-2012 to 2018-2022) and the coefficient for modelled transportation noise, in a joint regression of log complaint rate with liquor licences, density, poverty and borough as covariates. Complaints were counted from 311 (`erm2-nwe9`) for 2022 to 2024: 1.05 million residential complaints and 0.62 million street, sidewalk and vehicle complaints in 2,171 tracts. This is fewer than the 2,208 tracts in the shootings analysis because tracts without both ACS change measures were dropped. Modelled noise came from the US DOT National Transportation Noise Map for 2020 (road, aviation and rail, 24-hour equivalent level). The DOT service publishes only rendered map tiles, so each pixel was assigned to its legend class by colour; the map leaves areas below 45 dB uncoloured, and these pixels were set to 40 dB.

The registered contrast was -0.03 (-0.09 to 0.02) log units per SD, so the verdict is unsettled. Rent change had no association with residential complaints (0.004 per SD, -0.036 to 0.043). Modelled noise had a weak association (0.04 per SD, -0.003 to 0.077), and its Spearman correlation with the residential complaint rate was 0.22, below the registered ceiling of 0.25. The largest coefficients in the joint model were poverty (0.21 per SD) and the change in the non-Hispanic white share (0.13). The registered hypothesis named white-share change as a second measure of neighbourhood change, but the registered rule for support used only rent change. The contrast of white-share change minus modelled noise, reported as secondary, was 0.10 (0.04 to 0.15). Street and vehicle complaints showed a different pattern. They rose with modelled noise (0.13 per SD, 0.09 to 0.17; Spearman 0.30, as predicted) and with on-premises liquor licences (0.26 per SD, 0.22 to 0.30). Traffic and licensed premises are the noise sources that these two datasets describe.

The registration made three predictions: a weak correlation of residential complaints with modelled noise, a positive correlation of street complaints with modelled noise, and a positive contrast. The first two were met and the contrast was not, so the prediction was scored partial. Whether residential complaints measure noise exposure remains open, because these complaints are mostly about neighbours and the DOT map does not model neighbour noise. Measured sound levels could answer it. Possible sources are SONYC sensor series, if released at block level, a city monitoring campaign, or dosimetry at sampled addresses stratified by complaint rate and demographic change. With measured levels, the complaint rate can be split into sound level and reporting propensity. Propensity can be estimated from repeated reports of the same incident, as in the 2025 Annals of Applied Statistics paper on 311 reporting bias (doi 10.1214/24-aoas2003). No public SONYC sound-level file was found in a search on 12 November 2026.

## Rodents and child asthma (nbhd-rodents-asthma, confirmed)

The findings in the graph come from household studies and concern mice. Among mouse-sensitised urban children with asthma, each tenfold increase in mouse allergen in bed dust was associated with 1.87 times the odds of asthma-related health care (1.21 to 2.88; Torjusen et al. 2013, PMID 23067271). Among inner-city preschool children in Baltimore, high mouse allergen exposure in sensitised children was associated with an odds ratio of 2.1 (1.1 to 4.1) for an ED visit (Matsui et al. 2006, PMID 17069107). Olmedo et al. (2011, J Allergy Clin Immunol) found more mouse allergen in homes in NYC neighbourhoods with high asthma prevalence than in homes in low-prevalence neighbourhoods.

The registered hypothesis was that across the 42 United Hospital Fund (UHF) neighbourhoods, a higher share of homes with mice or rats in the building is associated with higher asthma ED rates among children aged 5 to 17. The rodent measure comes from the 2017 NYC Housing and Vacancy Survey. The ED rates are for 2017, and poverty (2013-2017) and renter housing problems (2017) are the covariates. All of these were taken from the EHDP data repository at commit 547482b. The registered prediction was a strong unadjusted correlation (0.5 to 0.8), most of which adjustment would remove. The model was an OLS regression of the log ED rate on the standardised rodent share, with HC3 standard errors.

The unadjusted Spearman correlation was 0.84. After adjustment the coefficient fell by 31%, from 0.68 to 0.47 (0.06 to 0.87) log units per SD. The interval excludes zero, so the verdict is confirmed. The coefficient did not fall as far as predicted, so the prediction was scored partial. With the cockroach share added, the coefficient was 0.53. The coefficient for adult ED visits was 0.41 (0.03 to 0.78). The design is weak. There are 42 units, and the rodent measure is almost collinear with housing problems (Spearman 0.90) and closely related to poverty (0.70). Race and ethnicity, smoking, outdoor air pollution and access to primary care were not adjusted for.

The city's operational rodent data do not measure what the housing survey measures. These checks used child asthma ED rates for 2023 and poverty for 2019-2023, with the same model and covariates. The share of DOHMH initial inspections failed for rat activity in 2022 to 2024 (`p937-wjvj`) had a Spearman correlation of 0.19 with the survey measure, and its adjusted association with asthma was -0.08 (-0.21 to 0.05). Rodent complaints to 311 per resident had Spearman correlations of 0.38 with the survey measure and 0.34 with asthma. After adjustment, the association with asthma was 0.06 (-0.11 to 0.23). Inspections and complaints mostly record rats outside and around buildings, while the research concerns mouse allergen indoors. The NYC measure that corresponds to the research is therefore the housing survey, not the inspection or complaint data.

Household data could test the link directly, for example NYC KIDS survey microdata on rodents in the home and asthma attacks, or a cohort with measured allergen, analysed with neighbourhood poverty and race as covariates. A test of rodent abatement, such as asthma ED rates before and after the Rat Mitigation Zones began, would test it within neighbourhoods.

## Measures that do not measure exposure

Two of the three analyses gave the same result. Records produced by residents' reports or by inspection programmes, such as 311 complaints and rodent inspections, follow the exposures studied in the research less closely than survey or model data do. Residential noise complaints follow poverty and ethnoracial change more than modelled noise. Rodent inspection failures do not follow the survey measure of rodents in homes. Analyses that treat these operational records as measures of exposure should validate them first against a direct measure. The research-needed entries in the outcome files list the data and designs.
