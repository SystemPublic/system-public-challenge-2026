# Affordability, gentrification and displacement in New York, 2000 to 2024

This note answers three questions. What does affordability show about different parts of the city? Do cheap places become expensive, in the pattern called gentrification? Where do the people who leave go, and who gains? For two tests the prediction and the conditions for support and refutation were committed to the project's public repository before the outcome data were read, so that the conditions could not be fitted to the result (`afford-gentrification-prevalence`, `afford-poor-count-shift`). These are called registered below. Every other number here is exploratory and is marked as such.

Both registered results were reviewed by a referee on 13 November 2026 and are stated here as revised (`critique/afford-gentrification-prevalence.md`, `critique/afford-poor-count-shift.md`). Eight of the 22 lowest-rent areas of 2000 gentrified under the fixed definition. This is a measurement and not a blind prediction, because a distorted view of the result was seen before registration. The count ranges from 5 to 12 under 35 other versions of the definition, and the 22 low-rent areas cannot be told apart from the other 33 (36% against 18%, p = 0.21). The number of poor residents fell more in the gentrified areas. That result passes its registered condition narrowly, is fragile, and concerns the change of a count: it is not evidence that residents were displaced.

## Data

The unit is the sub-borough area, a group of census tracts that approximates a community district. There are 55, each with 100,000 to 250,000 residents. The panel is the NYU Furman Center's CoreData file of 5 May 2026, which holds census 2000 and American Community Survey (ACS) values for 2000 and for each year from 2005 to 2024, in constant dollars. Other sources are marshals' executed evictions (NYC Open Data `6z8x-wfk4`), the Zillow Observed Rent Index by ZIP code, and Internal Revenue Service county migration files. The System Public Graph (a database of findings extracted from published research, called the graph below) was searched by variable name with 246 requests. After findings that use the search words in a medical sense were removed, it returned 326 findings in 146 studies, of which 33 findings in 15 studies name gentrification.

The file has one fault that matters. The 2022 ACS redrew the survey areas from which the sub-borough areas are built, and the population of 28 of the 55 areas changes by more than 8% between 2021 and 2022. Medians and shares are less exposed to this than counts are. Both registered tests used 2022-2024 as the end period, and both were repeated afterwards with 2017-2019.

## Affordability now (exploratory)

Between 2000 and 2022-2024 the city's median gross rent rose 41% in constant dollars, from $1,330 to $1,877, and its median household income rose 11%, from $74,750 to $83,117. The median renter paid 26.2% of income in rent in 2000 and 31.1% in 2022-2024. In 2000 the median renter paid more than 30% of income in 3 of the 55 areas, and in 2022-2024 in 39.

Rent takes the largest share of income where rents are lowest. The median share is 40% in Kingsbridge Heights/Mosholu, where the median rent is $1,467, and 24% in Park Slope/Carroll Gardens, where it is $2,900. Across the 55 areas the rank (Spearman) correlation of the median rent share with median income is -0.73. Low rent does not make an area affordable to the people who live in it (Figure 3a).

A household at the city median income can pay $2,078 a month at 30% of income. The median rent of all tenants is within that budget in 40 of 55 areas, the same count as in 2009-2011. The median rent of tenants who moved in recently is within it in 18 of 55 areas in 2020-2024, against 30 of 55 in 2006-2010. Zillow's asking rents for October 2025 to September 2026 are within it in 1 of 136 ZIP codes; the median asking rent is $3,013. A household at half the median income has $1,039, and no area's median rent is within that in either period (Figure 3b). Tenants who stay pay less than tenants who arrive; long tenure and rent regulation are the likely reasons, and this note does not test them.

## Is gentrification real? (registered)

The definition was fixed in advance. The low-start group is the 22 areas (the bottom 40%) with the lowest median gross rent in 2000. An area gentrified if its rent grew faster than the city's and its share of adults with a college degree rose by more points than the city's, from 2000 to the mean of 2022 to 2024. The registered readings were "common" (12 or more of 22), "myth" (1 or fewer, or no positive association between rent growth and college growth across the 55 areas) and "real but a minority" (4 to 11, with a positive association and at least 3 when growth is measured from 2005-2007). The registration computed in advance that if rent growth and college growth were unrelated the last reading would be reached with probability 0.02, so the test could fail in that direction. The referee found that it could hardly fail in the other. The city's rent growth and college growth are above the median area's, so only 18 of the 55 areas exceed the city on rent and 21 on college share, and 12 of 22 passing both was difficult to reach.

Eight of the 22 gentrified (36%, 95% interval 20% to 57%), and the reading is "real but a minority" (confirmed). The count is also 8 when growth is measured from 2005-2007. An area chosen for an unusually low rent estimate in 2000 would show spurious growth from 2000 and none from a later baseline, so this result argues against that explanation (regression to the mean). The Spearman correlation of rent growth with college growth across the 55 areas is 0.68 (0.50 to 0.80). The eight are Williamsburg/Greenpoint, Bedford Stuyvesant, Bushwick, North Crown Heights/Prospect Heights, Brooklyn Heights/Fort Greene, Central Harlem, Morningside Heights/Hamilton Heights and Washington Heights/Inwood. The first five, all in Brooklyn, rose 32 to 38 places in rent rank, for example Bedford Stuyvesant from 6th cheapest to 43rd. The three in upper Manhattan passed the rent condition by 1 to 9 points and moved 4 to 8 places (Figures 1 and 2). No Bronx area qualified. The other 14 low-rent areas kept their rank: Mott Haven/Hunts Point was the cheapest area in 2000 and is the cheapest now. Six of the 33 higher-rent areas also met both conditions, among them Astoria, Sunset Park and Park Slope. That is 18% against 36%, and Fisher's exact test of the two shares gives p = 0.21. The data show that about a third of low-rent areas had rent and college share rise faster than the city's. They do not show that this happened more often in low-rent areas than elsewhere.

The count depends on the rule and the membership depends on it more. The referee varied the rent condition in seven ways and the college condition in five, and over the 35 combinations the count ranges from 5 to 12, with 34 of the 35 between 5 and 11. With other end periods it is 6 to 10; with 2017-2019, before the boundary change, it is 9 (South Crown Heights is added) and the correlation is 0.53. The five Brooklyn areas are in the set under every end period tried and pass the rent condition by 42 to 96 points of growth. The three upper Manhattan areas pass it by 8.7, 7.9 and 0.8 points, and Washington Heights/Inwood, at 0.8, leaves the set in five of the ten other end periods. The supported statement is that five areas changed by a wide margin and that two to five more are near the threshold.

The prediction was not blind. Before registration I saw a distorted version of the outcome. A code test multiplied the real later values by random noise when it should have replaced them, and its output showed a count of 6 and a correlation of 0.38. The referee rebuilt that test with 400 other seeds: a typical distorted set contains about 5 of the 8 true areas, and its count is between 4 and 11 in 79% of draws. The preview was therefore informative. The registration says that the thresholds and the script were written before that run. The repository cannot confirm the order, because no commit precedes the registration and the file the test wrote is no longer present. The referee judged it unlikely that the thresholds were fitted to the preview, since the window of 4 to 11 is wider than a preview of 6 would suggest. The verdict stays confirmed because the registered conditions are met. The result should be read as a measurement under a fixed definition, and the prediction is no longer scored as a hit.

The graph holds no finding on how often low-rent neighbourhoods gentrify. The studies it holds report 18% to 33% under other definitions, which is the range of three shares and not an interval: 5 of 28 block groups in Durham (PMID 36367672), 23.8% of urban tracts nationally (PMID 37728889) and 14 of 42 New York neighbourhoods (PMID 37018221). Two of them report that the sign of the health association changes with the definition (PMID 37678111: odds ratios of 0.89 and 1.18 for severe maternal morbidity under two definitions).

## Who gained, and where did people go?

The registered test compared the change in the number of residents below the poverty line. It fell 29% in the eight gentrified areas (341,000 to 241,000), 10% in the other 14 low-rent areas and 3% in the other 33. The registered measure is the difference between the two groups in the mean of the logarithm of the ratio of the later count to the 2000 count, and the condition for support was a 95% upper limit below zero. The difference is -0.28 (-0.54 to -0.02), n = 22. The condition is met by 0.02, a narrow pass (confirmed).

The referee found the pass fragile. Removing any one of six areas puts the upper limit at or above zero, as does taking 2023 or 2024 alone as the end year, or moving Washington Heights/Inwood or East Harlem, which are 0.8 and 2.0 points from the rent threshold, to the other group. The point estimate is negative in every variant. The main cause is the 2022 boundary change. A count is a rate multiplied by a population, so it takes the whole change, and the standard error of the difference is 0.121 for 2022-2024 against 0.050 for 2017-2019. On 2017-2019 the difference is -0.24 (-0.35 to -0.14), and the poverty rate fell 6.5 points more in gentrified areas (4.4 to 8.6). That period was chosen after the data, so it is exploratory and does not decide the verdict. Two further limits apply. Seven of the 14 comparison areas are in the Bronx, where no area gentrified, and with borough held constant the registered difference is -0.17 (-0.48 to 0.13) and the 2017-2019 difference is -0.17 (-0.29 to -0.04). The gentrified areas adjoin one another, so the 22 areas are not independent and the intervals are too narrow by an unknown amount. Most of the difference arose between 2010-2012 and 2017-2019.

A falling count of poor residents cannot show displacement. In the gentrified areas the number of residents above the poverty line rose 25% (728,000 to 907,000) while the number below it fell 29%. The same figures would result if poor residents moved out, if they died or aged and were replaced by residents who are not poor, if the incomes of residents who stayed rose past the poverty line, or if households were counted in another area after the boundary change. The gentrified group is also defined partly by a rising college share, and graduates are seldom poor, so some of the association follows from the definition.

The comparison of the two groups of low-rent areas is exploratory (8 against 14 areas; intervals from a t test with unequal variances).

The table describes areas and not people. A rise in an area's median income is consistent with new residents who earn more, and does not show that the original residents' incomes rose. For low-income renters (households at or below 80% of the area median income), no difference in the change in rent burden was detected between the two groups, and the intervals are wide. Eviction rates are lower in the gentrified areas, and were lower in 2017-2019 as well (5.5 against 11.2). Violent crime fell there and rose slightly in the other low-rent areas. The project's health indicators from the Environment and Health Data Portal are for a single period on other boundaries, so no health change was measured.

Open data do not follow people, so displacement itself was not measured, and three partial measures were used. First, between 2000 and 2017-2019 the number of poor residents fell by 256,000 citywide, and the eight gentrified areas, with 20% of the poor residents of 2000, account for 94,000 of the fall. The count rose in 12 of 55 areas by 30,000 in total, most in Flushing/Whitestone, Hillcrest/Fresh Meadows, Mott Haven/Hunts Point and Morrisania/Belmont (Figure 4). No part of the city gained poor residents on the scale that the gentrified areas lost them. The data cannot say whether those residents left the city, died, or stopped being poor. Second, the share of residents born in New York State fell 3.6 points more in gentrified areas (0.7 to 6.5), which indicates more newcomers there. Third, the IRS files show where people who leave a borough go and what they earn. In 2021-2022 the 9,300 people who moved from Manhattan to the Bronx had a mean adjusted gross income of $50,000 per return, against $316,000 for those who stayed in Manhattan and $280,000 for those who moved to the nearby suburbs. Lower-income leavers from Manhattan go to the Bronx and Queens, and lower-income leavers from Brooklyn go to Queens and Staten Island. These are borough totals and cannot be tied to gentrified neighbourhoods.

## What the research says, and what the graph lacks

The disputed question is whether low-income residents leave gentrifying neighbourhoods at a higher rate than they leave other poor neighbourhoods. Freeman and Braconi (2004) and Dragan, Ellen and Glied (2020) found no higher rate in New York. Neither study is in the graph, and no finding in the graph compares exit rates between neighbourhood types. The graph does hold findings on the consequences. Among 12,882 residents of gentrifying New York neighbourhoods with hospital records, 23% were displaced in eight years, and their rate of emergency visits and hospitalisations, mainly for mental health, was 1.8 times that of residents who remained (1.5 to 2.2; PMID 29272306). Serious psychological distress fell among white residents of the fastest-gentrifying New York neighbourhoods and not among Black or Latino residents (PMID 37018221). Eviction in New York was followed by loss of Medicaid coverage (odds ratio 1.63, 1.38 to 1.92; PMID 35000688). Nationally, gentrifying tracts had 62% more firearm injuries than similar tracts (PMID 37728889), while violent crime fell in New York's gentrified areas. The two results use different measures and comparison groups.

The PubMed abstracts of the twelve studies cited or checked for this note were read. One record stores a percentage as a ratio. Graetz and colleagues (PMID 38007965) report mortality 40% higher after an eviction judgment and 12% higher at a rent burden of 70% than at 30%, and the graph stores these as hazard ratios of 40 and 12 in place of 1.40 and 1.12.

## Limits

The Census API now requires a key, so no panel of census tracts was built, and fifty-five large areas hide change inside them, and Washington Heights and Morningside Heights pass the definition narrowly. Median rent covers regulated and public-housing tenants, so it understates what a newcomer pays in the Lower East Side or East Harlem. The national poverty line takes no account of rent. The eviction counts omit the 6% of records without a community district. Zillow's index covers 136 of the city's ZIP codes and only 62 since 2015. A tract panel on fixed boundaries and records that follow individuals would answer what this note leaves open.
