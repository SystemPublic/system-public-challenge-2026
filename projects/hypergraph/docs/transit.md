# Transit claims

Each section is one registered claim about the subway, buses or for-hire vehicles.

## transit-gtrain-substitution: did app trips replace the G train in summer 2024?

The published research disagrees about whether ride-hailing replaces public transit or adds to it. Hall, Palsson and Price (2018) found that Uber raised transit ridership by five percent after two years in the average US metropolitan area. Graehler, Mucci and Erhardt (2019) found heavy rail ridership falling 1.3% for each year after ride-hail entry, and Erhardt and colleagues (2022) attributed a 10% fall in San Francisco bus ridership to it. These studies compare cities over years. The G train shutdown offers a test inside New York over weeks: from 28 June to 3 September 2024 the MTA closed the line in three phases for signal work, and free shuttle buses replaced the trains.

The claim was registered and pushed to GitHub (branch `prereg/transit-gtrain-substitution`) before any trip or ridership values were queried. The task had given the year as 2023; the announcements place the shutdown in 2024, so the registration uses the 2024 trip records and 2023 as the placebo year. The registered prediction was a rise of 0.02 to 0.12 log points in daily Uber and Lyft pickups in seven taxi zones on the days when a station served only by the G had no trains, relative to 32 Brooklyn and Queens zones within 5 km that have no G station.

The closure is clear in the subway data. Entries at the stations coded closed fell 98.9% from their June mean during their closed phase (MTA Subway Hourly Ridership, data.ny.gov wujg-7c2s), and the same days in 2023 show no closure.

Daily pickups rose by 0.043 log points, about 4.4%, with a zone-clustered 95% interval of 0.001 to 0.084 (4,680 zone-days; TLC High Volume FHV trip records for June to September 2024). The same model on 2023 gives -0.002 (-0.031 to 0.027). The estimate is inside the predicted range and the placebo is null. The registration also required a permutation test, because seven treated zones are too few for clustered standard errors to be trusted. In 2,000 draws, seven control zones chosen at random were given the treated schedules; 19% of the draws produced a coefficient at least as large as 0.043. That condition for support failed, and the condition for refutation (an interval lying below 0.02) failed too. The verdict is unsettled.

The registered sensitivity checks agree in sign. The three zones that depend most on the G (Greenpoint, Bedford, Clinton Hill) give 0.072 (0.011 to 0.132). The triple difference with 2023 gives 0.045 (0.006 to 0.083), and a Poisson model of counts gives 0.016 (-0.022 to 0.054). By zone the estimate is largest in Greenpoint (0.12), where the G is the only line, and near zero in Williamsburg South Side (-0.005), where the L stops at the same complex. That ordering is what substitution predicts, but it was not a registered test.

The size matters more than the sign. The estimate implies about 55,000 extra pickups against 1.14 million entries lost at the closed stations, or about 5 app trips per 100 lost entries. Most displaced riders took the shuttle bus or another line, or did not travel. The project's earlier claim commute-fhv-subway-distance found fewer app pickups per resident in zones farther from the subway. The two results are compatible: places far from the subway have more cars and lower density, and when a station closes for some weeks only a small share of its riders move to app trips.

Three limits apply. The trip counts came from TLC's monthly parquet files, which hold the same records as NYC Open Data bqxa-629f and u253-aew4, because the portal timed out on the aggregation; the registration named this fallback. The reference period contains a weekend (8 and 9 June 2024) on which entries at the G-only stations fell by about three quarters, which indicates an earlier closure. The System graph could not be queried from this machine, and the cached evidence has no concept for ride-hailing or the subway.

A test that could settle the question needs pickups located more finely than a taxi zone, or a second event. The G was cut again on nights and weekends from 14 July to 18 August 2025, and the same model can be registered for it with this result as the prediction.

The figure is `figures/transit/gtrain_shutdown_pickups.png`, the code is in `analyses/transit/gtrain_*.py`, and the results are in `data/derived/transit/gtrain_results.json`.

## transit-fhv-price-subway: did subway entries rise when for-hire trips in Manhattan became more expensive?

If ride-hailing draws riders from the subway, a higher price for ride-hailing should send some of them back. New York has raised that price twice in a defined area. From 2 February 2019 the state charged $2.75 on each for-hire vehicle trip and $2.50 on each taxi trip that touched Manhattan south of 96th Street. From 5 January 2025 the Congestion Relief Zone toll added $1.50 to each high-volume for-hire trip and $0.75 to each taxi trip in the zone south of 60th Street. The 2019 surcharge was registered as the primary test because it fell on for-hire and taxi trips only. The 2025 toll also charged private cars, so it was registered as a secondary, descriptive comparison.

The research disagrees about the direction. Hall, Palsson and Price (2018) found transit ridership 5% higher two years after Uber entered a metropolitan area. Graehler, Mucci and Erhardt (2019) found heavy rail ridership falling 1.3% a year after entry, and Diao, Kong and Zhao (2021) found transit ridership 8.9% lower. On the surcharge itself, Li and Vignon (2024) report ride-hailing trips down about 11% and yellow taxi trips down about 8%. The only study found of its effect on the subway is an undergraduate thesis (Dilworth 2023), which reports no significant rise. The System graph could not be queried from this machine; the cached evidence holds no concept for ride-hailing or the subway.

The registration was committed in `f14bcec` and pushed alone to GitHub (branch `prereg/transit-fhv-price-subway`, commit `f179bce`) before any ridership or 2018-2019 trip counts were queried. It predicted a rise of +0.05% to +1.3% in weekday entries inside the area relative to outside, most likely about +0.2%, and said in advance that a test with a standard error above 0.1 points could not detect the central value. It also set a gate: if for-hire and taxi pickups inside the area did not fall relative to outside, the verdict would be no_test.

The gate failed, and the verdict is no_test. The registered first stage compared weekday pickups by taxi zone, eight weeks after the surcharge against three weeks before, less the same change in 2018 (TLC monthly trip files, January to March 2018 and 2019). Between 11% and 19% of for-hire trips had no pickup zone in three of the four windows, so the registered rule passed the gate to taxis alone, and taxi pickups inside the area changed by +1.8% (−0.8 to +4.6) relative to outside. The combined series gives −1.1% (−2.4 to +0.3). The pickup comparison is a weak measure of the treatment, because a trip from an airport to Midtown pays the surcharge although its pickup is outside the area. This weakness was in the registered plan.

The registered subway estimate is −0.7% (−2.0 to +0.6), from 77 station complexes south of 96th Street and 331 elsewhere in Manhattan, the Bronx, Brooklyn and Queens (MTA Subway Hourly Ridership 2017-2019, data.ny.gov `t69i-h2me`). The placebo, the same comparison for 2018 against 2017, gives −0.5% (−1.1 to +0.2). The estimate is negative against upper Manhattan alone (−0.9%) and against the other boroughs alone (−0.7%). Night entries, when for-hire trips are a larger share of travel, give +2.2% (−0.4 to +4.8). The smallest effect this design could detect is about 1.9%, which is above the largest effect registered as plausible. Had the gate passed, the verdict would have been unsettled.

One exploratory figure, not registered, shows the size of the problem. For-hire and taxi trips touching the area numbered 590,902 a weekday in the three weeks before the surcharge and 569,645 in the eight weeks after; in the same weeks of 2018 they were 565,502 and 569,898. That is a fall of 4.3%, about 25,700 trips a weekday, with no comparison area. If a quarter of those riders moved to the subway, zone entries would rise by about 0.17% on a base of 2.48 million. The subway comparison has a standard error of 0.68 points and cannot see a change of that size.

For the 2025 toll, entries at complexes in the zone changed by −1.2% (−2.8 to +0.3) relative to all other complexes, January to March 2025 against autumn 2024, less the same change a year earlier (`wujg-7c2s`, `5wq4-mkjj`). The placebo for 2024 against 2023 is +1.4% (−0.2 to +3.2). Against Manhattan north of 60th Street the estimate is −0.2% (−1.9 to +1.4). The project's claim mob-crz-traffic found high-volume for-hire trips in the zone down 14.3% relative to outside in 2025, and speeds up 5.7%; repro-mmr-cbd-speed found that the speed gain against Manhattan north of 60th Street was 2.6% in January to June 2025 and absent after that. The subway entries show no matching relative rise inside the zone. Entries are counted where riders board, so a commuter who stops driving adds one entry outside the zone and one inside it, and this comparison is not a count of trips into the zone.

Together with commute-fhv-subway-distance and transit-gtrain-substitution, the result places a bound on what station entries can show. The trips that the per-trip charges removed are a small fraction of subway entries in the same area, and a station-level comparison of entries cannot detect them. A rider panel, or the MTA origin-destination estimates for the 2025 toll, could.

The figure is `figures/transit/fhv_price_subway.png`, the code is in `analyses/transit/fhv_price_subway*.py`, `register_fhv_price_subway.py` and `outcome_fhv_price_subway.py`, and the results, with every query and file version, are in `data/derived/transit/fhv_price_subway_results.json`.

## Weekday pattern of subway entries in the Manhattan CBD (transit-weekday-pattern)

Verdict: confirmed. The claim was registered in commit `b696a08` and pushed to the branch `prereg/transit-weekday-pattern` before any ridership value was read.

The question was whether Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday are now about equal in Manhattan activity, whether Monday and Friday are lower, and whether that gap was absent before 2020. The measure is fare-paid subway entries from the MTA Subway Hourly Ridership datasets on data.ny.gov (`t69i-h2me` for 2017 to 2019, `wujg-7c2s` for 2020 to 2024, `5wq4-mkjj` from 2025), summed over the 65 station complexes that the MTA station table (`39hk-dx4f`) flags as inside the central business district. Entries are a proxy for people present. They are not a count of pedestrians, and exits are not recorded.

Each weekday's entries were divided by the mean of Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday in the same week. Weeks containing a federal holiday or any day from 24 December to 1 January were dropped, which left 42 weeks in 2019 and 41 weeks in the 12 months from November 2025 to October 2026. Intervals are 95% intervals from resampling weeks. The registration defined "about equal" as a range of at most 3 points among the three midweek days, "lower" as a Monday/Friday deficit of at least 8%, and the comparison with 2019 as a rise in that deficit of at least 4 points.

| Entries as % of the Tue to Thu mean | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 93.6 | 98.7 | 100.3 | 101.0 | 96.2 |
| Nov 2025 to Oct 2026 | 83.6 | 98.4 | 101.2 | 100.4 | 88.8 |

In the recent period Monday and Friday together are 13.8% below midweek (12.3 to 15.9). Monday is 16.4% below (13.2 to 20.9) and Friday 11.2% below (10.3 to 12.1). The three midweek days lie within 2.77 points of each other (1.8 to 4.1), so the 3-point condition is met by a small margin. In 2019 the Monday/Friday deficit was 5.1% (4.3 to 6.0). The gap was therefore present before 2020, as the registration predicted, and it has grown by 8.7 points (6.9 to 10.9). The placebo, the same change from 2018 to 2019, is 0.74 points (−0.82 to 2.53).

The recent Monday mean includes two Mondays, 26 January and 23 February 2026, with entries at 38% and 20% of that week's midweek level. The cause was not checked. Using the median over weeks, the recent Monday deficit is 12.9%, the Monday/Friday deficit is 12.0% and the rise from 2019 is 7.5 points (6.7 to 8.3). The other registered checks give rises of 10.6 points for 25 complexes near Grand Central and Wall Street, 8.0 points when only the holidays themselves are dropped, 7.6 points for calendar 2025, and 8.0 points as the mean over complexes; the deficit rose at 64 of the 65 complexes. The deficit was 10.4% in 2022, 14.1% in 2023, 13.0% in 2024 and 12.7% in 2025.

By hour of entry, the deficit rose most in the morning peak (from 7.9% to 23.1%) and the evening peak (from 7.1% to 19.6%). The registration predicted that the evening peak would show the largest rise, and that was wrong. Friday mornings are now 31% below midweek, against 13% in 2019. Midday entries on Fridays are above midweek in both periods, and Friday entries after 20:00 are 9% above midweek now and were level with it in 2019. For morning-peak entries at all stations in the Bronx, Brooklyn and Queens the deficit rose by 9.4 points (7.0 to 12.1), more than the 2 to 6 points registered and less than in the CBD peaks. The busiest complexes are nearly the same as in 2019: 19 of the top 20 are in both lists, and the rank correlation over 424 complexes is 0.98. Seventeen of the top 20 are CBD complexes, against 16 in 2019, where the registration predicted the same number or fewer.

The research side rests on survey evidence that days worked from home rose to about four times their 2019 share (Barrero, Bloom and Davis 2023, doi:10.1257/jep.37.4.23) and on travel-diary evidence about Fridays from England (Motte-Baumvol, Schwanen and Bonin 2024, doi:10.1016/j.trd.2024.104245). We found no peer-reviewed measurement of the weekday pattern in fare-collection data against a pre-2020 baseline; the pattern is asserted in MTA statements and in Kastle's office-occupancy reports, which are proprietary. The System graph could not be queried from this machine, and the cached responses contain no finding on this edge. The design shows the pattern and its change. It does not show the cause: the MTA reduced Monday and Friday service on some lines from 2023, and visitors are a different share of entries than in 2019. The implication for shops and restaurants cannot be tested with open data, because no open NYC dataset gives sales by day of the week and small area.

The figure is `figures/transit/weekday_pattern.png`, the code is `analyses/transit/weekday_pattern.py` with `register_weekday_pattern.py` and `outcome_weekday_pattern.py`, the results are in `data/derived/transit/weekday_pattern.json`, and every query with its dataset update time is in `data/derived/transit/weekday_pattern_sources.json`.

## Dose map: change in subway entries by station complex since 2019

The dose map is step 1 of `docs/consequence_map.md`. It measures exposure only, and no outcome data (crime, health, crashes, taxi trips, restaurants) were queried for it. For each of the 424 station complexes in the MTA hourly ridership data it gives mean entries by day of week and hour band (night 00-06, AM peak 06-10, midday 10-16, PM peak 16-20, evening 20-24) in calendar 2019 (`t69i-h2me`, 42 retained weeks) and from November 2025 to October 2026 (`5wq4-mkjj`, 41 retained weeks), with the same week-exclusion rule as the weekday claim, and the recent value as a ratio of the 2019 value. Each complex also carries its Monday and Friday deficit relative to the Tuesday-to-Thursday mean in both periods and the change, the all-week ratio, the weekday-midday ratio, the weekend ratio, its coordinates and borough, the taxi zone (`8meu-9t5y`), 2020 NTA (`9nt8-h7nd`) and community district (`5crt-au7u`) containing it, and the MTA central business district flag. All 424 complexes fall inside a polygon of each layer. The files are `data/derived/transit/dose_map.csv` (one row per complex), `dose_map_long.csv` (complex by weekday by band), `dose_map_sources.json` (every query, dataset update times and boundary file hashes) and `dose_map_summary.json`; the code is `analyses/transit/dose_map.py` and the figure is `figures/transit/dose_map.png`.

The deficit is reported for the 420 complexes with at least 20 usable weeks and at least 500 mean Tuesday-to-Thursday entries a day in both periods. Their median Monday/Friday deficit rose from 2.7% to 6.9%, a change of 4.0 points (middle half 2.9 to 5.4; range -8.9 to +19.1), and 411 of the 420 rose. The median change was 7.8 points at the 65 CBD complexes and 3.8 points at the other 355. These are medians over complexes, and so they differ from the 5.1% to 13.8% for the CBD total in the weekday claim, which weights complexes by entries. Twenty-one complexes thinned on Mondays and Fridays combined by more than 10 points, of which 19 are in Manhattan, all of the 21 except Clinton-Washington Avs (Brooklyn) and Broadway N/W (Queens) being CBD complexes. The largest is Canal St (1) at 19.1 points, then Spring St (C,E), 34 St-Hudson Yards, Houston St and WTC Cortlandt (14 to 16 points). Counted by day, 38 complexes thinned on Mondays by more than 10 points and 17 on Fridays; 13 did both and 42 did at least one. Nine complexes have a negative change, the largest being Astoria Blvd (-8.9) and Mets-Willets Point (-6.1).

Entries have not recovered to 2019 levels in most places. The median all-week ratio is 0.76 (middle half 0.68 to 0.84) and the citywide weekly total is 0.78 of 2019. Weekday-midday entries have a median ratio of 0.78 (0.71 to 0.88), and 35 complexes are above 1. No community district has a weekday-midday ratio above 1; the highest are Brooklyn 10 (0.92), Queens 5 (0.91) and Brooklyn 6 (0.91), and the lowest are in the Bronx (0.60 to 0.66). Of 116 NTAs with at least 2,000 weekday midday entries a day in 2019, three have ratios above 1: Astoria (North)-Ditmars-Steinway (1.12), North Corona (1.06) and Williamsburg (1.02). Weekend entries (Saturday and Sunday together) have a median ratio of 0.87 and are above 2019 at 115 complexes. Fifteen of 57 community districts gained weekend entries, led by Brooklyn 1 (1.27), Manhattan 3 (1.26) and Manhattan 6 (1.20). Among NTAs, East Village (1.79), Williamsburg (1.76), Astoria (North) (1.58) and Gramercy (1.39) gained most. The complexes with the largest weekend ratios are mostly on the L, which carried reduced weekend service in 2019, and on the N and W in Astoria, where a ratio of 6.2 at Astoria Blvd suggests a 2019 suspension; this was not checked, and weekend and midday ratios above 1 at single stations may reflect service changes or station closures in 2019 and not a gain in activity. The 4 complexes dropped from the deficit measure are small, and ratios for complexes with few entries are unstable.

The map shows the thinning on Mondays and Fridays concentrated in the Manhattan business district, with the smallest changes at outer-borough complexes, and shows weekday-midday entries below 2019 almost everywhere. Entries are fare-paid boardings and not people on the street, a complex may be affected by service changes between the two periods, and the mean over retained weeks excludes weeks with holidays.
