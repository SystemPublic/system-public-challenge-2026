
## Trash containers and rat sightings in Manhattan Community District 9

Registered as further-containers-rats-cd9 (commit 6d74c65, pushed alone as prereg/further-containers-rats-cd9) and further-repro-dsny-rat-sightings (commit 850ce0e), before any rat counts were read. DSNY release 25-042 of 16 December 2025 says that putting millions of pounds of trash in sealed containers "has also sent rats fleeing", and reports 25,230 rat sightings to 311 from December 2023 to November 2024 and 20,025 a year later. The test asked whether 311 rat sightings in Manhattan CD9, fully containerised from 2 June 2025, fell more than in the adjacent districts 7, 10, 11 and 12, comparing June 2025 to May 2026 with the previous twelve months. The comparison districts were chosen by rule, not by matching on pre-period levels.

The citywide figures reproduce approximately. From complaint type Rodent and descriptor Rat Sighting, the table fetched on 12 November 2026 gives 25,519 and 20,196 sightings, 1.15% and 0.85% above the stated counts, and a decline of 20.9% against a stated 20.6%. All twelve months from December 2024 to November 2025 are below the same month a year earlier. The registered tolerance of 1% was missed on the first count by 0.15 points, so the verdict is unsettled.

In CD9 311 rat sightings fell from 492 to 380 (22.8%). In the four comparison districts they fell from 3,214 to 2,439 (24.1%). The difference in differences is a rate ratio of 1.02 (95% interval 0.86 to 1.23, month-pair bootstrap). The placebo year (June 2023 to May 2024 against the year before) gives 1.08 (0.89 to 1.36). The registered prediction was 0.75 to 0.95 and the verdict is unsettled: neither the support condition nor the refutation condition was met. The result is consistent with no extra fall in CD9 and does not exclude an extra fall of 14%. Windows of 6 and 17 months give 1.05 (0.81 to 1.44) and 0.98 (0.84 to 1.15). Using each comparison district alone gives 0.85 (CD11) to 1.19 (CD12), and using all other Manhattan districts gives 0.95 (0.80 to 1.14). Residential noise complaints, a placebo type, give 1.07 (0.99 to 1.15).

The DOHMH measure, the share of initial inspections with active rat signs, fell from 15.3% to 14.8% in CD9 and from 19.7% to 17.7% in the comparison districts, an odds ratio of 1.09 (0.90 to 1.36). Initial inspections in the comparison districts fell 6% while CD9's were unchanged, so inspection effort differs between them.

311 records complaints by residents. A fall in complaints shows fewer calls, and shows fewer rats only if reporting behaviour did not change. A sceptical referee would first point to the low power (about 380 sightings a year in CD9), the lack of parallel pre-trends (the series cross several times before 2025), and the unknown pilot-zone boundary, which means CD9 boundaries stand in for the treated area. The question would be settled by block-level bin start dates and a measure of rats that does not depend on calls. Figure: figures/further/containers_rats_cd9.png. Outcome records: claims/outcomes/further-containers-rats-cd9.json and further-repro-dsny-rat-sightings.json.

## Electricity demand in New York City (NYISO Zone J)

Registered as further-zonej-demand-growth (commit 15737cc, pushed alone as prereg/further-zonej-demand-growth) before any load value was read. NYISO's 2026 Power Trends says that "electricity demand is increasing and becoming more uncertain". The test asked whether weather-normalised annual energy in Zone J (N.Y.C.) grew by more than 0.5 percent a year over 2015 to 2025, with a 95 percent interval above zero, and whether the summer peak also rose. The load is the 5-minute integrated real-time load that NYISO publishes (132 monthly files), regressed day by day on heating and cooling degree days at Central Park, with day-of-week, holiday and month effects. The days from 15 March 2020 to 30 June 2021 were excluded by a rule fixed in advance.

The registered refutation condition was met. The Zone J energy trend is -0.75 percent a year (95 percent interval -0.84 to -0.67, 3,521 days) and the weather-normalised summer peak trend is -0.78 percent a year (-0.94 to -0.62). Weather-normalised annual energy was 51.9 TWh in 2015, 52.7 in 2016, 51.8 in 2019, 49.2 in 2021, 48.7 in 2023 and 49.8 in 2025. The verdict is contested. Every specification that uses the whole period gives a similar estimate (-0.74 to -0.77 in the pandemic-indicator, drop-2020-2021, alternative-base-temperature, LaGuardia-weather and month-by-cooling checks, and -0.89 to -0.66 by 30-day block bootstrap).

The period divides into three parts. Over 2015 to 2019 the trend is -0.09 percent a year (-0.30 to +0.11), over 2022 to 2025 it is +0.56 (+0.32 to +0.81), and between them the level fell by about 5 percent from 2019 to 2021. The full-period negative trend therefore comes mostly from that fall, not from continuing decline, and a linear trend does not describe the series well. These sub-period fits were registered as sensitivity checks and do not decide the verdict. The 2022 to 2025 rise is above the registered 0.5 threshold in its point estimate, but its interval reaches 0.32.

The Long Island placebo (Zone K) falls at -0.86 percent a year (-0.97 to -0.75), so the decline is not specific to New York City; Zone J minus Zone K is +0.11 points a year (+0.02 to +0.19). A second placebo, which gave the model weather from three years earlier, reduced the share of variance explained by weather from 0.238 to 0.004. The load totals agree with the Gold Book's own historical Zone J energy within 1 percent in every year from 2016 to 2025.

Gold Book forecasts were compared with realised growth for editions 2021 to 2025. Forecast growth minus realised growth at a three-year horizon was +3.0, -1.9 and -4.0 percentage points for the 2021, 2022 and 2023 editions (mean -1.0, bootstrap interval -4.0 to +3.0), so the forecasts were neither biased high nor biased low by the registered rule. Only three editions have a three-year horizon, and the 2021 edition starts from the pandemic year 2020.

A referee would first object that the full-period linear trend is fitted across a level shift whose cause is not identified: it could be a lasting loss of commercial load, behind-the-meter solar (the measured load is net of it) or efficiency, and the data here cannot separate them. The daily-model intervals are narrow because they rest on thousands of days, while the trend is informed by 9 to 11 years; an unregistered annual-level interval is wider (-1.11 to -0.42) but also excludes zero. Con Edison sales by class, behind-the-meter solar by zone and an occupancy series would settle the cause. Figure: figures/further/zonej_demand_growth.png. Analysis: analyses/further/. Results: data/derived/further/.

## Data centres in Manhattan's electricity

Registered as further-datacentre-share-manhattan (commit f7ed2f9, pushed alone as prereg/further-datacentre-share-manhattan, 6af48a3) before any electricity, floor-area or data-centre value was read. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report for the Department of Energy (December 2024) says data centres "consumed about 4.4% of total U.S. electricity in 2023" (176 TWh; quote checked on the LBNL summary page). The test asked whether data-centre use is a smaller share of Manhattan's benchmarked electricity than 4.4 percent in calendar year 2024. The registered upper bound (U) was the whole-building grid electricity of ten carrier hotels and colocation buildings named from public sources before the data were read, plus every Manhattan building reporting any data-centre floor area, as a share of all Local Law 84 grid electricity (D1) and of Con Edison non-residential electricity in Manhattan ZIP codes from the Utility Energy Registry (D2). The verdict was set at U against both denominators.

The registered refutation condition was met. D1 is 17.06 TWh over 10,647 Manhattan rows, U is 2.76 TWh, and U is 16.2 percent of D1 (95 percent interval 15.5 to 16.9, bootstrap over the other buildings, so it reflects composition and not reporting error) and 17.2 percent of D2 (16.03 TWh). The verdict is contested. The result comes almost entirely from the second component of U. The ten listed buildings alone hold 0.38 TWh, 2.2 percent of D1 and 2.4 percent of D2. The other 165 buildings with a positive data-centre floor area hold 2.38 TWh; 78 of them report less than 5,000 square feet of data-centre space, and they include hospital and laboratory campuses and large offices whose whole consumption is counted in U. The registered rule counts these whole buildings, so U is a loose bound that cannot fall below 4.4 percent. The listed-only figure is a registered sensitivity check and does not decide the verdict.

The measures built on reported data-centre fields are small. Reported data-centre floor area is 6.8 million square feet of 1.73 billion square feet of reported floor area, 0.39 percent (0.38 to 0.41). Reported IT-equipment energy, from 26 buildings, is 0.14 TWh, 0.83 percent of D1 (0.79 to 0.86) and 0.88 percent of D2. Data-centre area share applied to building electricity gives 1.5 percent of D1 and 1.6 percent of D2. No 2024 row reports a power usage effectiveness, so the PUE-scaled bound could not be computed for 2024 (it gives 0.49 percent for 2023). Other years and variants of U against D1: 15.8 percent for 2023, 17.0 for 2022, 20.1 without campus child rows, 20.6 after dropping rows with estimated energy or a data-centre alert (D1 falls to 6.6 TWh), and 16.2 net of on-site generation.

The placebos show that the whole-building rule inflates the share of any rare use. Laboratory buildings (62) hold 0.59 percent of floor area and 4.6 percent of D1 by whole-building electricity, above the 4.4 percent line for a use that is not a data centre. Eleven size-matched Manhattan offices, drawn 2,000 times, hold 0.62 percent of D1 (2.5th to 97.5th percentile 0.44 to 0.96), so the listed set's 2.2 percent is well above what ten large buildings hold without a data centre. In the Bronx, where no carrier hotels are listed, 12 buildings report data-centre area (Verizon central offices, a public safety answering centre); their whole buildings hold 1.7 percent of Bronx D1 (3.76 TWh), the data-centre area share is 0.23 percent and no building reports IT energy.

Coverage is limited. Local Law 84 covers large buildings (the two dataset descriptions give 25,000 and 50,000 square feet), and 1,290 Manhattan rows had no usable grid electricity value. Tenants of carrier hotels often meter their own electricity, so the landlord's figure may omit most data-centre load, and 60 Hudson Street's own row was missing for 2024: the join on its lot returned the 56 Hudson Street property (use type Data Center, 1.03 million square feet, 0.09 TWh), and the Western Union Building row for 2022 and 2023 was not used. D1 includes residential buildings above the size threshold and so is not the same population as D2 (non-residential only; total consumption is 18.33 TWh, which gives 15.0 percent). In D2, 197 of 809 ZIP-month cells are zero or blank, which can only raise the shares against it. The 4.4 percent comparator is national, so it does not describe an expected Manhattan share.

A referee would first object that the registered upper bound is uninformative, because counting whole hospitals and offices makes the contested verdict a property of the rule. The question would be settled by metered data-centre load: Con Edison account-level or service-class data for the listed buildings, tenant-metered Portfolio Manager IT meters, or Local Law 97 reports by space type. Figure: figures/further/datacentre_share_manhattan.png. Analysis: analyses/further/datacentre_*.py. Results: data/derived/further/datacentre_results.json.

## Chinatown, Sunset Park and Flushing, 2010 to 2020 (further-chinatown-shift)

The claim is taken from AALDEF's report "Mapping Chinatown" (4 December 2025), which gives Manhattan Chinatown's non-Hispanic Asian and Pacific Islander population as 52,613 in 2010 and 41,024 in 2020, a fall of 22 percent. The report takes its 2020 figure from the 2018-2022 American Community Survey, not from the 2020 census, and describes its boundary in words only. The registration, committed before any population count was read, treated this as a reproduction with tolerances of 5 percent on each count and 3 points on the change, and added directional predictions for Sunset Park and Flushing. It was pushed alone to the branch prereg/further-chinatown-shift.

The primary reading used the 23 census tracts of the Chinatown and Lower East Side neighbourhood tabulation areas. It gives 48,749 residents in 2010 and 38,279 in 2020 (ACS margin of error 2,551), a change of -21.5 percent. The change is inside the tolerance, but both counts are 7 percent below AALDEF's and outside it. None of the 16 registered readings (three boundaries, five definitions, two tract mappings) reproduces all three numbers, and the verdict is contested. The same tracts have 120,801 residents in 2010 against AALDEF's 116,722, with more Black and Hispanic residents and fewer Asian residents than AALDEF reports, so AALDEF's boundary differs from the neighbourhood areas.

The 22 percent depends on comparing the 2010 census with the ACS. Between the 2010 and 2020 censuses the same tracts lose 10.3 percent of their Asian and Pacific Islander residents (tract bootstrap 95 percent interval -17.1 to -0.7); the Asian-alone change is -10.3 percent (-17.0 to -0.6). The ACS estimate for 2020 is 12 percent below the census count in the same tracts, by more than its margin of error. A 2020 undercount of Asian residents would make the census decline smaller, not larger. Random sets of 23 Manhattan tracts south of 96th Street gained 23.5 percent on average (5th to 95th percentile 4.3 to 44.6), and none declined as much as the Chinatown set. Manhattan's Asian-alone population rose 23.6 percent and the city's 33.4 percent.

In Sunset Park the 8 tracts along 8th Avenue gained 9.6 percent in Asian-alone residents (23,327 to 25,555) and the other 23 tracts gained 29.3 percent (21,482 to 27,780). The difference is -19.8 points (95 percent interval -28.0 to -11.4), as registered, but the corridor did not lose Asian residents, as the AAF 2021 brief says for 2010 to 2019. Sunset Park as a whole gained 19.0 percent (13.0 to 25.7) and Flushing 21.0 percent (13.3 to 32.5). The placebo does not isolate the Asian pattern cleanly: Hispanic residents changed by +12.0 points more in the core than the periphery (3.0 to 24.6), while total population differed by -1.3 points (-5.3 to 3.0).

The Asian-weighted centroid of Manhattan south of 14th Street moved 22 m east between 2010 and 2020, and the northernmost majority-Asian tract (36061004100) is the same in 2000, 2010 and 2020. The count of majority-Asian tracts was 7, 8 and 6. The intervals come from resampling tracts and describe variation between tracts; the census counts are complete counts, with 2020 disclosure-avoidance noise that changes the set estimates by under 0.5 points under the assumed noise levels. The Census API required a key, so keyless bulk files (PL 94-171, the ACS table-based summary file) were used. Chinese-alone counts and 1990 data were not obtained. The figure is figures/further/chinatown_shift_map.png.

## Do 311 flood reports follow the tide? (further-tidal-flooding-311)

The claim was that on rain-free days, 311 street-flooding complaints from the tidal ZIP codes (Howard Beach, Broad Channel and the Rockaways, Red Hook, Coney Island) rise on days when the Battery water level reaches the NOAA minor-flood level, and rise more than in other ZIP codes. The registration was committed before any complaint count was read (commit c3e4870; outside copy on branch prereg/further-tidal-flooding-311, commit 153e2dc).

The registered test could not be run. It required at least 30 rain-free days at or above the minor-flood level (2.104 m above MLLW) and at least 100 street-flooding complaints in the tidal ZIPs on rain-free days. The sample of 1,268 rain-free days from 1 January 2020 to 31 October 2026 contains 20 such days (3 in 2021, 3 in 2022, 14 in 2024), and 291 complaints. The verdict is no_test.

The secondary estimates, which do not decide the verdict, are in the predicted direction. In the tidal ZIPs the rate ratio for a flood day was 15.7 (95 percent block-bootstrap interval 4.3 to 33.4); in the other ZIPs it was 1.50 (0.88 to 2.10); the ratio of the two was 10.5 (2.3 to 27.4). The mean was 0.19 complaints a day on other rain-free days and 2.65 on the 20 flood days. The estimate rests on a few episodes, mostly 19 to 22 September 2024, and falls to 5.5 on 6 flood days when 2024 is left out. A continuous tide measure gave a rate ratio of 1.19 per 10 cm (1.06 to 1.32) in the tidal ZIPs and 1.01 (0.98 to 1.04) elsewhere. With catch basin and sewer backup complaints added, the tidal rate ratio was 4.0 (1.7 to 7.5).

The placebos do not support the model. Assigning the tide of 7 days later gave a tidal rate ratio of 0.13 (0.00 to 0.42), where the registration expected about 1. The 100-day shift gave 1.25 (0.42 to 2.39). The result at 7 days shows that the year and month terms absorb the September 2024 cluster, so the baseline is unstable with so few events. The FloodNet sensors flagged as tidally influenced recorded 189 events in 491 sensor-days on rain-free flood days and 649 in 26,358 on other rain-free days (rate ratio 15.6, 13.3 to 18.4, counting sensors as independent); the other sensors recorded 1 event in 1,997 and 21 in 133,948.

A referee would first attack the small number of flood days and their clustering in one autumn, then the use of two airport rain gauges to define rain-free days, then the Battery as the exposure for Jamaica Bay. The question would be settled by more rain-free flood days, for example from the 2010 to 2019 311 file, and by matching FloodNet events to individual complaints. The figure is figures/further/further-tidal-flooding-311.png.

## Subway felonies per entry by hour of day (further-subway-crime-per-ride-by-hour, further-subway-crime-per-million-repro)

The Governor's release of 18 December 2025 states 1.65 major crimes per million riders in 2025, down roughly 30 percent from 2021, and says nothing about hours of day. Two claims were registered and pushed to the branch prereg/further-subway-crime-per-ride-by-hour before any count was queried. The first reproduces the number; the second asks whether late-night rates per entry differ from daytime rates and whether they have returned to 2019 levels.

The reproduction is contested under its registered readings. The MTA Major Felonies table (yeek-jhmu) gives 1.11 felonies per million riders for the New York City Transit rows from January to November 2025 (1,755 felonies over an implied 1,578 million riders), and a fall from 2021 of 27.0 percent. None of the six registered readings gives both 1.65 and a fall of 27 to 33 percent. A recomputation that the registration labelled secondary, NYPD felony complaints at subway premises over MTA subway entries, gives 1,940 complaints over 1,186 million entries, or 1.64 per million for January to November 2025, against 2.33 for the same months of 2021, a fall of 29.9 percent. This matches the release, but it was not one of the registered readings and decides nothing; it is a candidate for a new registration.

For the hourly question the numerator is NYPD felony complaints of the seven MTA major types with premise "TRANSIT - NYC SUBWAY" and the denominator is MTA estimated subway entries, for 2019 and for October 2025 to September 2026. The all-day rate per million entries was 1.48 (95 percent Poisson interval 1.42 to 1.53) in 2019 and 1.59 (1.52 to 1.66) in the latest 12 months, a ratio of 1.08 (1.02 to 1.14). By band, the latest rates were 17.7 (16.2 to 19.2) from midnight to 5 am, 1.04 (0.93 to 1.15) from 5 to 10, 1.18 (1.07 to 1.29) from 10 to 16, 1.02 (0.93 to 1.13) from 16 to 20 and 2.11 (1.88 to 2.35) from 20 to 24. The midnight to 5 am rate is 15.0 (13.3 to 17.0) times the 10 to 16 rate, and it was 16.1 (14.4 to 18.0) times in 2019. That band holds 2.4 percent of entries and 26 percent of complaints.

The registered verdict is unsettled. The first support condition, a night to day ratio of at least 3, was met by a wide margin. The second, that the midnight to 5 am rate is above its 2019 level, was not: the ratio of the latest rate to the 2019 rate is 0.97 (0.86 to 1.08), and the night ratio relative to the all-day ratio is 0.90 (0.79 to 1.02) against a registered threshold of 1.10. The all-day rate per entry is itself above its 2019 level, so the premise that it had returned was not true. The 5 to 10 band has the clearest rise, 1.26 (1.08 to 1.46) times its 2019 rate. Calendar 2025 in place of the latest 12 months, a wider premise, transit-district assignment, dropping complaints timed exactly 00:00 or 12:00, violent types only and shifted bands do not change the verdict. Non-subway felonies of the same types shifted toward the 0-5 band by a factor of 1.05 (1.02 to 1.07), under the registered threshold of 1.10.

A referee would first attack the denominator. Fare entries do not count people on trains and platforms, so a per-entry rate at night overstates risk per person-hour if night riders stay longer, and the hourly ridership is a modelled estimate whose error is not in the intervals. The second attack would be the numerator: complaint times are reported times, premise coding may have changed after 2020, and complaints are reports. The comparison of a calendar year with a 12-month window crosses seasons, which the calendar 2025 check addresses. The result does not measure perceived safety; the survey figures (22 percent feeling safe at night) were read only through news summaries.
