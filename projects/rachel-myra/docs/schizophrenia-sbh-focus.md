## 1. Illness or medication? What drives heat-wave deaths in schizophrenia

**Short answer: both, plus poverty and isolation. Studies can't fully separate them, but the evidence leans toward medication adding real risk on top of the illness.**

### Evidence that the illness itself (and its circumstances) matters
- **Bark 1998 (Bronx Psychiatric Center / Einstein)**
  - New York State psychiatric hospital patients had about twice the heat-wave death risk of the general NYC population: relative risk 1.38 vs 1.17, 1971–84.
  - Prevention measures introduced in 1979 brought patients' risk down from 1.49 (1971–79) to 1.13 (1980–84). The author concluded these deaths are **preventable**.
  - A Psychiatric Times summary says excess risk existed **before** antipsychotics, which would suggest the illness alone is a factor. That claim hasn't been checked against the original paper, and sources disagree on the study period.
  - [Psychiatric Services 49:1088](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ps.49.8.1088); [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/heatstroke-and-psychiatric-patients)
- **Wong 2024 (Japan, within-person design)**
  - Heat-wave illness risk was nearly the same whether patients were taking antipsychotics or not: IRR 1.48 vs 1.45, interaction p=0.53.
  - This is the strongest evidence that the **illness** carries the risk. PMID 39648665.
- **Cincinnati 1999 heat wave (Kaiser 2001)**
  - Mental illness OR 14.0 for heat death.
  - Toxicology suggested many who died **weren't taking** their psychotropics.
  - A working air conditioner was the strongest protection (OR 0.03).
- **Reviews:** people with schizophrenia may have thermoregulation problems that come from the disease itself, but the direct experiments are sparse. Unmedicated sweating has never been measured. ([Sci Total Environ 2025 review](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969725016055))

### Evidence that medication adds risk
- **Chen et al., Sci Rep 2025: 2021 BC heat dome case-control study** ([PMC12494884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12494884/), DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-17591-0)
  - 137 people with schizophrenia who died vs. 57,394 who survived. **All had schizophrenia, so the illness is held constant.**
  - Adjusted for illness severity: ED visits, admissions, relapse.

  | Exposure | Adjusted OR [95% CI] |
  |---|---|
  | Any antipsychotic | **2.43** [1.52, 4.01] |
  | Monotherapy | 1.79 [1.08, 3.05] |
  | **2+ antipsychotics** | **4.05** [2.41, 6.98] |
  | Haloperidol | 3.31 [1.45, 6.57] |
  | Clozapine | 2.63 [1.53, 4.40] |
  | Zuclopenthixol | 2.58 [1.12, 5.22] |
  | Risperidone | 1.71 [1.06, 2.67] |
  | Olanzapine | 1.60 [1.01, 2.45] |
  | Aripiprazole | not elevated |
  | Income assistance (poverty) | 2.45 [1.65, 3.68] |
  | Male sex | 1.67 [1.17, 2.40] |

  - Most people who died were alone at home.
  - The authors say antipsychotics are "just one factor," that their results are "not clinical," and that they **cannot** support stopping antipsychotics, which are lifesaving.
- **French 2003 heat wave:** antipsychotics aOR 2.09 for death (ages 70–100) and OR 4.6 for heat admission (Martin-Latry 2007). In the System graph, Martin-Latry is filed as "psychotropic drugs."
- **US Medicare (Layton 2020):** antipsychotics RR 1.37 for heat hospitalization.

### How to reconcile it
- The **Japan** result compares each person with themselves and finds no added heat-wave effect from the drug.
- The **BC** result compares people with the same illness and finds a dose-response pattern: monotherapy 1.79, combination 4.05.
- **Combination therapy and the specific drugs** (haloperidol, clozapine, zuclopenthixol high; aripiprazole not) fit a drug mechanism: anticholinergic and dopamine-blocking effects reduce sweating and thirst.
- **But combination therapy also signals more severe illness.** No study fully separates the two.

### What this means for the product
- For schizophrenia, the label should show **FLAG = high** (this patient needs heat outreach regardless of the drug) and **CHANGE = moderate**.
  - Combination therapy and the high-risk drugs are worth reviewing **before** summer.
  - Never stop abruptly.
- The strongest single protective factor in the data isn't a drug change. It's **access to cooling and someone checking in**.

## 2. SBH Health System (St. Barnabas), the Bronx

**What it is:**
- Safety-net hospital serving mostly Medicaid and uninsured patients.
- St. Barnabas Hospital: 4422 Third Ave, Bronx 10457, in the Belmont/Tremont/Fordham area.
- Network includes SBH Ambulatory Care and SBH Behavioral Health (260 E 188th St, 10453).
- Sources: [SBH locations](https://www.sbhny.org/locations/); [AHA case study](https://www.aha.org/system/files/media/file/2022/10/SBH-Health-System-MAH-Case-Study.pdf)

**Behavioral health scale, directly relevant to schizophrenia:**
- SBH Behavioral Health is one of the largest mental health providers in the Bronx, with 160,000+ visits a year.
- 49 adult inpatient psychiatric beds.
- Psychiatric emergency care inside an ED that sees about 100,000 visits a year.
- Adult and child outpatient clinics.
- A medical-psychiatric unit for patients with both acute medical and psychiatric needs.
- Sources: [SBH Psychiatry](https://www.sbhny.org/our-services/psychiatry/); [Bronx Times](https://bxtimes.com/sbh-health-system-medical-psychiatry-unit)

**Bronx heat context:**
- The NYC Heat Vulnerability Index places many of the highest-risk neighborhoods in the Bronx, Brooklyn and Queens.
- The 2026 NYC heat mortality appendix lists 12 heat-stress deaths among Bronx residents (15% of the city's total) over 2016–2025.
- The city estimates about 525 heat-related deaths a year citywide (2018–22), mostly at home.
- Sources: [NYC Heat Mortality Report](https://a816-dohbesp.nyc.gov/IndicatorPublic/data-features/heat-report/); [2026 appendix](https://a816-dohbesp.nyc.gov/IndicatorPublic/data-features/heat-report/2026-Heat-Mortality-Report-Appendix.pdf)

**The Bronx connection:** the landmark study on psychiatric patients dying in heat waves (Bark 1998) came from **Bronx Psychiatric Center**, and it found that prevention worked.

**Not found / to do:**
- SBH's 2025–27 Community Service Plan ([PDF](https://www.sbhny.org/fr/community-engagement/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/SBH-2025-2027-CSP.pdf)) lists its service-area zip codes and health needs, but its text couldn't be extracted. Open it by hand.
- No SBH-specific heat program or heat-related visit data was found.

### Ideas for the product
- **Make SBH's catchment the demo.**
  - Run the playbook for the zip codes around SBH (10453, 10457, 10458, 10460 and nearby; confirm against the CSP).
  - Show the Heat Vulnerability Index plus CDC PLACES rates of mental illness, diabetes and high blood pressure there.
- **Write the schizophrenia label for an SBH-style outpatient clinic:**
  - before summer: review combination therapy
  - during heat waves: a check-in call list
  - cooling-center locations near 188th St and Third Ave

## 3. The judge's point: heat changes how much drug a patient gets

**The judge's observation:** in heat or fever, the same dose can act stronger or weaker.

**What the evidence supports:**
- Vanakoski & Seppälä, *Clin Pharmacokinet* 1998, review (DOI 10.2165/00003088-199834040-00004, PMID 9571303):
  - **Pills** (midazolam, propranolol, tetracycline): heat changes absorption and elimination only a little.
  - **Skin patches and under-the-skin injections** (insulin, nitroglycerin, nicotine, fentanyl): heat **raises** blood levels, because skin blood flow can rise several-fold.
  - Human data are "very limited."
- **Stronger effect** in heat:
  - insulin absorbs faster, raising hypoglycemia risk
  - fentanyl and other patches release more drug
  - lithium, digoxin and dabigatran build up when dehydration reduces kidney function
  - blood pressure drugs add to heat's own blood-pressure drop
- **Weaker effect** in heat:
  - epinephrine, insulin and EMS drugs degrade in heat
  - patches peel off with sweat
  - inhalers deliver unreliably when used hot

**Why a numeric "safe dose for this temperature" algorithm isn't feasible:**
- No validated data exist to calibrate one. The insulin evidence is one study of 8 people in a sauna.
- Core temperature estimators from wearables have never been validated in medicated patients, and they miss many readings above 39°C.
- Giving patients a dose number would be unverified medical advice.

**What to build instead: a "Heat Dose Compass"** (clinician-facing):
- For each drug, an **arrow** for which way heat pushes exposure or effect (↑ stronger / ↓ weaker / ↕ depends).
- The **mechanism**: absorption, buildup, degradation, or added effect.
- The **trigger**: forecast heat, fever, exertion, or a heat source on a patch.
- **What to monitor**: levels, glucose, blood pressure, sedation.
- **Evidence strength**.
- An optional **body-temperature slider** shows how the risk grows. It's labeled as illustrative, never as a dose.
- This captures the judge's insight honestly. It also shows where the evidence runs out, which the brief rewards.
