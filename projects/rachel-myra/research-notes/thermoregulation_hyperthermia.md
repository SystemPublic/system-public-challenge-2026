# Rare and Extreme Drug–Heat Harms: Thermoregulation and Hyperthermia

**Scope note on evidence quality (applies throughout):** the great majority of "tail-end" drug–heat harms exist only as single case reports, small case series, or spontaneous-report (FAERS/postmarketing) signals. Where a controlled human trial or an epidemiologic design exists I have flagged it explicitly. Where I could not verify an identifier or find a source, I have said so in **Gaps** rather than guessing. All PMIDs/DOIs below were retrieved from PubMed/NCBI E-utilities during this research session.

---

## Q1. Drug-induced anhidrosis/oligohidrosis and heat stroke (topiramate, zonisamide, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, anticholinergics, glycopyrrolate, botulinum toxin)

### Takeaway
Carbonic-anhydrase-inhibiting antiseizure drugs (zonisamide, topiramate) are the best-documented cause of true drug-induced oligohidrosis, and the literature runs from asymptomatic sweat-test abnormalities in children all the way to fatal heat stroke and permanent cerebellar injury in adults; anticholinergics (notably oxybutynin) have their own small but striking heat-stroke case literature. Incidence estimates are on the order of 1 case per 4,600–10,000 patient-years, i.e. genuinely rare, which is why nearly all evidence is case-level.

### Cited Findings

**Zonisamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibition; postganglionic sudomotor failure)**
- Earliest extreme pediatric case: a 2-year-old boy with frontal lobe epilepsy on zonisamide developed a "heat stroke-like episode" with hyperpyrexia, oligohidrosis, **chorea-like involuntary movements, resting tremor and cogwheel rigidity**; pilocarpine iontophoresis showed markedly reduced sweat response indicating *postganglionic* sudomotor dysfunction, skin biopsy showed morphologically normal sweat glands, and sweating recovered within 2 weeks of stopping the drug. Evidence type: single case report with mechanistic sweat testing — [Shimizu et al., Brain Dev 1997; PMID 9253492](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0387-7604(97)00029-6)
- FDA/CDER review of FAERS found 6 US domestic cases of zonisamide-associated oligohidrosis and/or fever, **all in patients ≤18 years**, a reporting rate of **13 cases per 10,000 pediatric-years of exposure — roughly 10-fold the Japanese reporting rate**; proposed mechanism was carbonic anhydrase inhibition in eccrine sweat glands altering pH dynamics, H+ concentration and calcium transients, with pediatric age a risk factor via elevated blood levels relative to body size. Evidence type: pharmacovigilance case series — [Knudsen et al., Pediatr Neurol 2003; PMID 12770670](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0887-8994(02)00511-8)
- Pooled incidence estimates: **13 events of oligohidrosis/hyperthermia in the US in the 3 years after approval ≈ 1 case per 4,590 patient-years**; in Japan all reported cases over the first 11 years of marketing were in children, **≈1 case per 10,000 pediatric-years**; hyperthermia reports clustered in **summer months**; oligohidrosis was reversible on discontinuation in every case. Evidence type: review of postmarketing surveillance — [Low et al., Epilepsy Res 2004; PMID 15519129](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eplepsyres.2004.06.009)
- Prospective screening: in 16 patients newly started on zonisamide, acetylcholine stimulation testing at ~1 month predicted later oligohidrosis with **sensitivity 1.0 but a false-positive rate of 0.67** (oligohidrosis in 0/4 with normal response, 4/12 with decreased response). Evidence type: small prospective cohort — [Okumura et al., Pediatr Neurol 2000; PMID 10963972](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0887-8994(00)00144-2)

**Topiramate (shares carbonic anhydrase inhibition)**
- Index pilot study: a 6-year-old with partial complex seizures had recurrent hyperthermia 2 months after topiramate was added, with episodes tied to **environmental temperature and physical activity**; pilocarpine iontophoresis sweat output was **5% of age-matched controls**, and both fever and sweat quantity normalized off drug. In 13 further patients (age 1–37), **9 had reduced sweat quantity** (mean 0.089 vs. control 0.21 g/30 min), 8 of them children — but **only 3 had clinical heat-intolerance symptoms**, i.e. a marked test-symptom discrepancy. Evidence type: case + small pilot case-control — [Ben-Zeev et al., J Child Neurol 2003; PMID 12760427](https://doi.org/10.1177/08830738030180041001)
- **Fatal** case: a 40-year-old man on topiramate, divalproex and rosiglitazone/metformin found unresponsive and "very hot to touch"; **rectal temperature peaked at 43.2 °C**, skin "hot and dry," GCS 3, severe metabolic acidosis, acute renal failure and rhabdomyolysis; died ~2 hours after ED arrival despite aggressive cooling. Critically, **serum topiramate (8.8 µg/mL) and valproate (97 µg/mL) were both within therapeutic range** — this was not an overdose. Evidence type: single fatal case report — [Borron et al., Am J Emerg Med 2013; PMID 23993866](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2013.07.013)
- **Permanent neurologic sequelae**: first reported case of topiramate-associated hyperthermia leaving **residual cerebellar (ataxia, tremor) and cognitive dysfunction** in an adult; prior published cases had all been clinically mild and fully reversible. Evidence type: single case report — [Galicia et al., Clin Neuropharmacol 2005; PMID 15795554](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.wnf.0000159957.95134.1b)
- Adult heat stroke on a non-epilepsy indication: a 57-year-old man admitted febrile and comatose, diagnosed as topiramate-induced heat stroke after a full workup; topiramate had recently been prescribed **for essential tremor**. Authors note outcomes "range from total clinical recovery to persistent neurological dysfunction or death" and recommend evaluating for hypohidrosis in adults, especially in hot conditions. Evidence type: single case report — [Canel et al., J Med Case Rep 2016; PMID 27075033](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-016-0835-5)
- Imaging-documented case: heat stroke in an epileptic patient on topiramate followed with MRI including DWI/ADC measurement. Evidence type: single case report — [Clin Neurol Neurosurg 2013; PMID 23411048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clineuro.2013.01.005)
- Additional topiramate hypohidrosis/hyperthermia case reports spanning adults, children and migraine prophylaxis (useful for showing breadth of indication, not just epilepsy): adult hypohidrosis — [Epileptic Disord 2013; PMID 23773932](https://doi.org/10.1684/epd.2013.0568); children — [Turk J Pediatr 2012; PMID 23427516]; reversible hypohidrosis on topiramate for **chronic migraine** — [Headache 2010; PMID 20236344](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1526-4610.2010.01636.x); pediatric symptom survey — [Pediatr Int 2010; PMID 19549063](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-200X.2009.02912.x); electrophysiological sudomotor study in 2 children — [Clin Neuropharmacol 2008; PMID 19050411](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNF.0b013e3181646cf2); symptomatic vs. asymptomatic hypohidrosis in children — [Turk J Pediatr 2005; PMID 16363346]; heat stroke during topiramate treatment (Spanish) — [Med Clin (Barc) 2007; PMID 17537371](https://doi.org/10.1157/13102058)

**Anticholinergics / antimuscarinics**
- **Oxybutynin**, an antimuscarinic for neurogenic bladder, caused heat stroke in an elderly man admitted confused with **rectal temperature 40 °C and hot dry skin on the hottest day of the San Francisco summer**; notably he had been admitted **the previous summer with 41.1 °C**, i.e. a recurrent, seasonally patterned event. Described as the first reported oxybutynin heat-stroke case. Evidence type: single case report — [Adubofour et al., Ann Pharmacother 1996; PMID 8835047](https://doi.org/10.1177/106002809603000207)
- Oxybutynin heat stroke **layered on Parkinson's disease autonomic failure** — anticholinergic sweat blockade superimposed on PD-intrinsic perspiration dysregulation. Evidence type: single case report — [Ahmad et al., Cureus 2021; PMID 34055543](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.14701)
- Meta-analytic confirmation that the anticholinergic mechanism is real in controlled human heat exposure: drugs with **high** anticholinergic properties raised core temperature by **+0.42 °C (95% CI 0.04–0.79)** at air temperatures ≥30 °C with reduced sweating — but the authors caution the evidence is essentially **limited to atropine**, and drugs with *weak* anticholinergic effects showed no core-temperature effect. Evidence type: systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized heat-stress trials (35 studies, 353 participants) — [Hospers et al., EClinicalMedicine 2024; PMID 39513185](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102886)

**Antihistamines — a negative result worth reporting**
- A double-blind randomized crossover trial of **50 mg diphenhydramine** vs. placebo in 20 adults (30 °C, 35% RH, 2 h rest then 60 min fixed-heat-production walk) found **no difference** in whole-body sweat loss (396 vs. 406 g), sweating onset (13.3 vs. 13.5 min), steady-state local sweat rate, ΔT_rec (0.81 vs. 0.83 °C) or heart rate. This directly contradicts the common guidance that antihistamines blunt sweating and raise heat-injury risk, at least at this dose in healthy young adults. Evidence type: RCT (crossover) — [Newhouse et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2024; PMID 39140777](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003527)

**Comparator: non-drug acquired anhidrosis (useful for mechanism and for how bad sudomotor failure gets)**
- Acquired idiopathic generalized anhidrosis (AIGA): widespread non-segmental anhidrosis sparing palms/soles/axillae, in which **"heat stroke readily develops"**; commonly affects young males; 30–60% have comorbid cholinergic urticaria. Shows what a pure sudomotor-failure phenotype looks like clinically. Evidence type: clinical review — [Satoh, Curr Probl Dermatol 2016; PMID 27584965](https://doi.org/10.1159/000446781)

### Inferences
- The zonisamide/topiramate signal is almost certainly a **class effect of carbonic anhydrase inhibition on the eccrine sweat gland**, which predicts (but does not confirm) similar risk for acetazolamide, methazolamide and possibly high-dose sulthiame.
- Pediatric predominance is best explained by **dose-per-body-size pharmacokinetics plus a higher surface-area-to-mass ratio and greater reliance on evaporative cooling**, consistent with the FDA analysis attributing risk to elevated blood levels relative to patient size.
- The Ben-Zeev discrepancy (9/14 abnormal sweat tests but only 3 symptomatic) implies **drug-induced hypohidrosis is substantially under-recognized**, and that the tail-end heat-stroke cases are the visible fraction of a much larger latent-impairment population that only decompensates when heat + exertion load is added.
- The Borron fatality at *therapeutic* drug levels is the single most important point for a prescribing-risk argument: this is an idiosyncratic pharmacodynamic vulnerability, not a toxicity-dose phenomenon, so therapeutic drug monitoring offers no protection.

### Gaps
- **Acetazolamide**: despite being the prototype carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, I found **no case report of acetazolamide-induced anhidrosis or heat stroke**. My PubMed search ("acetazolamide hypohidrosis OR anhidrosis") returned only unrelated hits (congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis, Horner syndrome, ectodermal dysplasia). The acetazolamide link appears to be **mechanistic inference from the zonisamide/topiramate class effect, not documented in humans**. This should be stated as such in any report.
- **Glycopyrrolate**: I found **no human case report of glycopyrrolate-associated hyperthermia or heat stroke**. The only PubMed hits for "glycopyrrolate hyperthermia" were veterinary (post-anesthetic hyperthermia in cats) or malignant-hyperthermia anesthetic-protocol papers. Given glycopyrrolate's wide pediatric use for sialorrhea and its strong antimuscarinic action, the absence of reports is notable but I cannot assert a documented harm.
- **Botulinum toxin**: I found **no case report of botulinum-toxin-induced anhidrosis causing heat intolerance or heat stroke**. Searches returned only Harlequin syndrome cases and a potency study. Large-area botulinum treatment for hyperhidrosis is theoretically a focal sudomotor blockade, but I could not verify a published heat-harm case.
- I did not locate a FAERS/VigiBase disproportionality analysis specifically for drug-induced hyperthermia; the closest is a hypohidrosis/anhidrosis disproportionality study framed as drug *repurposing* for hyperhidrosis — [Eur J Med Res 2023; PMID 36829251](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40001-023-01048-z) — which I did not read in full and whose drug list I therefore cannot report.
- FDA label text and safety communications for topiramate/zonisamide oligohidrosis were not fetched; the pediatric oligohidrosis warning is referenced secondhand through the CDER authors of PMID 12770670.

## Q2. NMS, serotonin syndrome, anticholinergic toxidrome, MH-like reactions, parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia — does ambient heat, dehydration or heat waves trigger or worsen them?

### Takeaway
The toxidromes themselves are well described, and there is good evidence that **heat waves raise the risk of drug-associated heat illness in people taking antipsychotics and anticholinergics** (OR 4.6 and 6.0 respectively in the French 2003 heat wave). But I found **no study showing that ambient heat precipitates NMS, serotonin syndrome or parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome specifically** — the published triggers for PHS/DHS are pharmacologic (abrupt dopaminergic withdrawal, overdose, device failure), not environmental. This is an important honest negative.

### Cited Findings

**Heat waves + psychotropics: the strongest epidemiologic evidence**
- Matched case-control study during the August 2003 French heat wave: among 1,405 ED admissions, 56 (4%) had heat-related pathology (mean age 83). On multivariate analysis, cases were more likely to be on **anticholinergic drugs (OR 6.0, 95% CI 1.8–19.6), antipsychotics (OR 4.6, 95% CI 1.9–11.2), or anxiolytics (OR 2.4, 95% CI 1.3–4.4)**. Evidence type: matched case-control — [Martin-Latry et al., Eur Psychiatry 2007; PMID 17513091](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eurpsy.2007.03.007)
- Paris (Hôpital Européen Georges Pompidou) 2003 heat wave: of 165 heat-related ED patients, **31 (18.8%) died within one month**; **previous psychotropic treatment** was among the factors associated with short-term mortality, alongside dependency, admission temperature/HR, hypotension, hypoxia and altered mental status. Evidence type: retrospective cohort — [Davido et al., Emerg Med J 2006; PMID 16794091](https://doi.org/10.1136/emj.2005.028290)
- 1999 Cincinnati heat wave case-control (CDC): among 17 heat-death cases vs. 34 neighborhood controls, **mental illness carried OR 14.0 (95% CI 1.8–633)**; a working air conditioner was the strongest protective factor (OR 0.03, 95% CI 0–0.2); toxicologic screening suggested decedents **may not have been medication-compliant**, and 3 lived in group homes. Evidence type: case-control with death-scene investigation + toxicology — [Kaiser et al., Am J Forensic Med Pathol 2001; PMID 11563746](https://doi.org/10.1097/00000433-200109000-00022)
- New Orleans June 1998 heat wave ICU series: 8 heat-stroke admissions over 14 days (ambient >33.3 °C, heat index to 44.5 °C); **6/8 (75%) were on drugs known to induce or worsen hyperthermia** — most commonly cocaine, also diphenhydramine, TCAs and phenothiazines; 6/8 had rhabdomyolysis, 3 with DIC; **2 deaths (25% mortality)**. Evidence type: case series — [Martinez et al., South Med J 2002; PMID 12190212]
- Self-controlled case series in Japan (Tsukuba City claims, 2014–2019) — relevant for the cardiovascular/fluid-balance arm: heat-related illness rate rose more during heatwaves in person-time on antihypertensives (**IRR 1.64, 95% CI 1.53–1.75**) than off them (IRR 1.42, 1.27–1.58), with significant drug–temperature interaction (p<0.01); the signal held for **ACEi/ARBs and calcium channel blockers but not non-loop diuretics**. Authors explicitly caution this may be confounding by indication rather than a causal drug effect. Evidence type: self-controlled case series — [Harley et al., Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2026; PMID 42710533](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.70447)

**Antipsychotic/anticholinergic heat stroke — extreme individual cases**
- **Fatal exertional** heat stroke: a 36-year-old man with schizophrenia/bipolar disorder on zuclopenthixol, quetiapine, benztropine, carbamazepine and levothyroxine, **working as a roofer on the third day of a heat wave**, lost consciousness and presented with GCS 3 and **rectal temperature 42.2 °C**; progressed to severe multi-organ dysfunction and asystole, died the following morning. This case uniquely combines occupational exertion + heat wave + antipsychotic + anticholinergic + thyroid hormone. Evidence type: single fatal case report — [Kao & Kelly, Can J Clin Pharmacol 2007; PMID 18025545]
- **Recurrent** heat illness in the same patient in one summer: a 48-year-old man with diabetes and schizophrenia on benzhexol 2 mg bid, chlorpromazine 650 mg nocte and zuclopenthixol decanoate 600 mg q4wk was admitted **twice in summer 2004** after working in the sun in an open car park — first with heat stroke (14-day stay), then heat exhaustion (4-day stay) *despite* having been given heat-stroke precautionary advice. Authors attribute the combination of anticholinergic sweat inhibition plus antidopaminergic elevation of the hypothalamic set point. Evidence type: single case report with causality assessment — [Kwok & Chan, Ann Pharmacother 2005; PMID 16174785](https://doi.org/10.1345/aph.1G130)
- Heat stroke during maintenance olanzapine 20 mg + trihexyphenidyl 4 mg + trazodone 50 mg in a 49-year-old man with schizophrenia **during a heat wave**, requiring intensive care; the authors explicitly list NMS and serotonin syndrome as differential diagnoses, illustrating how hard these entities are to separate in practice. Evidence type: single case report — [Lee et al., Acta Neuropsychiatr 2015; PMID 26503496](https://doi.org/10.1017/neu.2015.29)
- Antipsychotic-induced hyperthermia leading to **cerebrovascular accident** — an unusual end-organ outcome. Evidence type: single case report — [Cureus 2021; PMID 34659926](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.18651)

**Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome (PHS) and dyskinesia-hyperpyrexia syndrome (DHS)**
- Comparative literature review plus 2 cases: **56 published PHS cases and 13 DHS cases**. Both occur mainly in **elderly, long-duration PD patients**, with **similar mortality** but different sex ratios and recovery times. Triggers are sharply different: PHS from **abrupt drug stoppage**, DHS from **drug excess/abuse**; treatment is drug reinstitution vs. drug reduction respectively. One index case had PHS **with cerebral salt wasting syndrome** after abrupt antiparkinsonian withdrawal. Evidence type: case reports + systematic literature review — [Wang et al., J Parkinsons Dis 2022; PMID 35811538](https://doi.org/10.3233/JPD-223362)
- DHS defined as severe continuous dyskinesia with **rhabdomyolysis, hyperthermia and altered mental state**; prognosis usually good "but sometimes fatal." Evidence type: case report + literature review — [Du et al., Front Neurol 2024; PMID 38249748](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2023.1323717)
- Unusual DHS triggers documented as case reports: **introduction of continuous subcutaneous levodopa infusion** — [Parkinsonism Relat Disord 2025; PMID 41061372](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.parkreldis.2025.108068); **istradefylline overdose** — [Rinsho Shinkeigaku 2022; PMID 35871565](https://doi.org/10.5692/clinicalneurol.cn-001740); **following subthalamic DBS** — [Mov Disord Clin Pract 2026; PMID 41992639](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.70646); DHS responding to **GPi DBS** — [Tremor Other Hyperkinet Mov 2025; PMID 40351565](https://doi.org/10.5334/tohm.912)
- Unusual PHS triggers: **rotigotine transdermal patch** — [Clin Park Relat Disord 2026; PMID 42109537](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prdoa.2026.100447); **interruption of levodopa by nutrition-support/NPO status** — [Nutr Clin Pract 2025; PMID 40223162](https://doi.org/10.1002/ncp.11297); **perioperative upper GI surgery** — [Qatar Med J 2023; PMID 38204562](https://doi.org/10.5339/qmj.2023.34); **"malignant deep brain stimulator withdrawal syndrome"** (device/battery failure) — [BMJ Case Rep 2019; PMID 31092485](https://doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2018-229122); PHS case series — [Cureus 2022; PMID 36320988](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.29646); NMS-like syndrome in **multiple system atrophy**, 3 cases — [BMC Neurol 2022; PMID 35216572](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-022-02583-8)
- Background susceptibility: thermoregulatory dysfunction is an **underrecognized feature of PD itself**, attributed to α-synuclein deposition in thermoregulatory structures, altered network connectivity and disrupted neurotransmitter modulation on top of aging — meaning PD patients start from a degraded baseline before any drug effect. Evidence type: narrative review — [Pressnell et al., Cells 2025; PMID 41369399](https://doi.org/10.3390/cells14231910)

**MH-like reactions, RYR1 and the EHS overlap**
- Knock-in mice expressing the RYR1 **p.G2435R** variant (isogenetic with the human p.G2434R, the commonest UK MH variant) showed **gene dose-dependent susceptibility to MH with volatile anesthetics *or to heat stroke from increased ambient temperature***, with elevated resting myoplasmic [Ca²⁺]i (123 → 156 → 265 nM across MHN/het/hom) and [Na⁺]i. This is the cleanest mechanistic demonstration that an MH genotype confers *environmental* heat-stroke susceptibility. Evidence type: animal model (mechanistic) — [Lopez et al., Br J Anaesth 2018; PMID 30236258](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2018.07.008)
- Calsequestrin-1 ablation produces Ca²⁺ imbalance and heat-stroke susceptibility — a second non-RYR1 excitation-contraction-coupling route to environmental heat vulnerability. Evidence type: animal/mechanistic — [Front Physiol 2022; PMID 36311237](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2022.1033300)
- Clinical overlap: EHS/exertional rhabdomyolysis survivors are routinely referred to **Malignant Hyperthermia Investigation Units**; in a Dutch cohort of 60 EHS/ERM patients (47 EHS, 13 ERM), self-reported long-term sequelae included muscle symptoms at rest (26%) or on exercise (28%) and **neurological sequelae (11%)**, with severe fatigue in 30% and mood/anxiety disorder indications in 11% on validated instruments; prehospital management was inconsistent with guidelines and 90% reported inadequate follow-up. Evidence type: prospective survey + retrospective record review — [Kruijt et al., Sports Med Open 2023; PMID 37204519](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00570-y)
- Conceptual synthesis arguing that **severe drug-induced hyperthermia (MH, NMS, serotonin syndrome) may converge on an inflammatory syndrome best described as heat stroke**, implicating amphetamines, diuretics, cocaine, antipsychotics, metoclopramide, SSRIs and TCAs. Co-authored by USARIEM's Thermal and Mountain Medicine Division. Evidence type: narrative review — [Horseman et al., Cureus 2022; PMID 36039261](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.27278)
- Dantrolene in classic/exertional heat stroke: some studies showed accelerated cooling when given after EHS onset, and benefit as pretreatment in animal models, but **accumulated data do not support routine use** as adjuvant cooling; use in severe or non-improving cases is "rational" but unproven. Evidence type: clinical review — [Hadad et al., Crit Care 2005; PMID 15693989](https://doi.org/10.1186/cc2923). See also the negative BET — [Emerg Med J 2008; PMID 18573966](https://doi.org/10.1136/emj.2008.061705) — and [Crit Care Med 1990; PMID 2302955](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003246-199003000-00009)
- Narrative review of **drug-associated non-pyrogenic hyperthermia** that deliberately *excluded* NMS and MH: of 2,609 records only **11 studies** met inclusion (5 case-control, 2 case series, 4 retrospective) — implicating psychotropics, anticholinergics, antihistamines, diuretics, cardiovascular agents, NSAIDs and anticoagulants; psychotropics were associated with increased heat-related mortality "other than through NMS," though findings varied. Evidence type: narrative review — [Bongers et al., Eur J Clin Pharmacol 2020; PMID 31642960](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-019-02763-5)

**Anesthesia-adjacent oddities**
- Anesthetic management of a patient with **both a past heat stroke and a past NMS episode** — illustrates how clinicians treat the two as a combined MH-risk phenotype. Evidence type: single case report (Japanese) — [Masui 2007; PMID 17877051]
- Atypical NMS **without hyperthermia or rigidity** in the ICU — relevant to how often these syndromes are missed or mislabeled. Evidence type: case report — [Acta Neurol Belg 2026; PMID 41995787](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13760-026-03068-y)

### Inferences
- The epidemiology supports a **"drug + heat wave + fixed environment" interaction** (no air conditioning, outdoor work, institutional living) rather than heat triggering a distinct toxidrome. The Kao roofer case and the Kwok car-park case both required *occupational exertion in heat on top of* the drug.
- Because antipsychotics blunt heat perception/behavioral thermoregulation as well as sweating, the dominant pathway in heat-wave deaths may be **failure to take protective action** rather than purely physiologic. The Cincinnati finding that air conditioning was overwhelmingly protective, and that decedents may have been non-compliant with their psychotropics, points the same way and complicates simple "drug causes heat death" attributions.
- The RYR1 mouse data imply a plausible but **unproven-in-humans** population of MH-susceptible individuals at elevated environmental heat-stroke risk; this is the most defensible mechanistic bridge between MH and EHS.

### Gaps
- **No study found showing ambient heat, dehydration or heat waves trigger or worsen NMS, serotonin syndrome, or PHS specifically.** Targeted searches ("neuroleptic malignant syndrome summer incidence", "serotonin syndrome heat stroke", "parkinsonism hyperpyrexia syndrome heat OR summer") returned no such study. Every documented PHS/DHS trigger I found is pharmacologic or device-related. This should be reported as a genuine evidence gap, not glossed as "heat probably contributes."
- No seasonal-incidence analysis of NMS located. This is a straightforward study that may simply not exist, or may exist outside PubMed's indexed English-language literature.
- The Stöllberger/Finsterer hypothesis that "thirst-blockers" (ACEi, sartans, SSRIs, dopamine agonists/antagonists, atypical neuroleptics) contributed to the French 2003 heat-wave death toll is a **letter to the editor, not primary data** — [Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2007; PMID 17960864](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.1456). There is a published reply (PMID noted in that record's "Comment in" as Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2008;17:312-3, doi 10.1002/pds.1548) which I did not read; the exchange should be characterized as hypothesis-generating only.

## Q3. Drug-associated exertional heat stroke in athletes, military recruits and workers

### Takeaway
The best-documented drug-linked exertional-heat deaths involve **sympathomimetic supplements** (ephedra, DMAA) and, in large military cohorts, there is a measurable association between heat illness and **methylphenidate stimulants, opioids and NSAIDs** — but the effect sizes are modest and limited to *mild* heat illness, while the dramatic individual cases are supplement- and stimulant-related. Caffeine's effect is real but small and ambiguous in direction for performance.

### Cited Findings

**Large military cohort (the best-powered medication evidence in this space)**
- US Army enlistees 2011–2014 (**N = 238,168**; 427,922 person-years): 2,612 mild heat illness (MHI) and 732 severe heat illness (SHI) cases. **71.3% of MHI and 60.2% of SHI occurred in the first 6 duty months, peaking at month 2.** Odds of MHI increased among those **taking NSAIDs, opioids, or methylphenidate stimulants**. Prior SHI quadrupled MHI odds (OR 4.02, 95% CI 2.67–6.03); BMI ≥30 raised SHI odds (OR 1.94, 1.47–2.56) and BMI <18.5 raised them too (OR 2.26, 1.16–4.39); tobacco use raised MHI odds 55% (1.37–1.77). Evidence type: large retrospective cohort with multivariable logistic regression — [Nelson et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2018; PMID 29613996](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001623)
- IDF Institute of Military Physiology, 174 EHI cases over Oct 2022–Sep 2025: **93.1% occurred in training, not combat**; 26.4% were classified as exertional heat stroke; the EHS proportion **rose to 36.7% during the acute-war phase** vs. 20.7% pre-war and 21.4% late-war (p<0.05). Strongest EHS predictor across the cohort was **insufficient sleep (OR 2.75, 95% CI 1.34–5.65)**, with wartime surges in exceeding effort scale, inadequate nutrition and late recognition of underperformers. Evidence type: cohort/registry analysis — [Simani et al., Mil Med 2026; PMID 42530530](https://doi.org/10.1093/milmed/usag350)
- Review of EHS epidemiology and mitigation in sport and military, noting that military surveillance is superior to sport surveillance because of standardized case definitions and mandatory reporting, while sport prevalence "is unclear due to inconsistent terminology, diagnostic criteria and data reporting"; medication and drug use is listed among susceptibility factors. Evidence type: narrative review — [Périard, DeGroot (Army Heat Center) & Jay, Exp Physiol 2022; PMID 36039024](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP090686)

**Supplements: the extreme tail**
- Two **active-duty soldier deaths** after ingesting multi-ingredient supplements containing **1,3-dimethylamylamine (DMAA)**; both collapsed during physical exertion from cardiac arrest and died. The authors draw the explicit parallel to ephedra. Evidence type: case reports (2) — [Eliason et al., Mil Med 2012; PMID 23397688](https://doi.org/10.7205/milmed-d-12-00265). Note this paper drew two published letters of rebuttal (PMIDs 24133750, 24133751), so causality was contested.
- **Marathon death associated with DMAA** use, investigated with clinical toxicology plus histopathology at Guy's and St Thomas'/King's College London. Evidence type: single fatal case report — [Archer et al., Drug Test Anal 2015; PMID 25641767](https://doi.org/10.1002/dta.1764)
- Cardiac arrest in a 21-year-old man after a DMAA-containing workout supplement; the specific product was **not on the FDA's list** of DMAA-containing products, which the authors flag as a surveillance failure. Reported DMAA adverse effects include cardiac arrest, hemorrhagic stroke and death. Evidence type: single case report — [Karnatovskaia et al., Clin J Sport Med 2015; PMID 24878759](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000103)
- Analytical characterization of four experimental stimulants in sports/weight-loss supplements: octodrine (2-amino-6-methylheptane), 1,4-DMAA, 1,3-DMAA and 1,3-DMBA — useful for showing the moving target of supplement sympathomimetics. Evidence type: analytical/toxicology study — [Clin Toxicol 2018; PMID 29115866](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2017.1398328)
- **Ephedra** supplement implicated in a sportsman's death (the Steve Bechler-era reporting). Evidence type: news/BMJ news item, **not primary research** — [BMJ 2003; PMID 12609922](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.326.7387.464/b)

**Stimulants and appetite suppressants**
- **Fatal heat stroke in an 11-year-old girl** taking two appetite-suppressant agents during a "nature hike" at 34 °C ambient, while **the other 107 participating children suffered no significant ill effects** — an unusually clean natural experiment isolating the drug. The authors give the dual mechanism: sympathomimetic amines **increase endogenous heat production via CNS stimulation and impede heat dissipation via peripheral vasoconstriction**. Evidence type: single fatal case report with internal comparison group — [Kew et al., S Afr Med J 1982; PMID 6128802]
- Fatal exertional heat stroke in a **15-year-old, 97-kg high school football player** after August practice: rectal temperature **107.3 °F (41.8 °C)**, BP 80/30, pulse 180, cooled in 40 min, then metabolic acidosis, raised liver enzymes, elevated troponin with prolapsed mitral valve, refractory hypotension despite ECMO; died after 3 days. The report specifically addresses the contribution of his **ADHD medication** and notes EHS is the third leading cause of death in US high school athletes. Evidence type: single fatal case report — [Allen & Cross, Pediatr Emerg Care 2014; PMID 25469604](https://doi.org/10.1097/PEC.0000000000000296)
- Severe hyperthermia after methylphenidate administration. Evidence type: single case report (brief) — [Peyre & Delorme, J Clin Psychopharmacol 2012; PMID 22388166](https://doi.org/10.1097/JCP.0b013e3182499677)
- ADHD-specific athlete considerations review. Evidence type: narrative review — [Sports Health 2018; PMID 29144831](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738117742906)

**Workers**
- OSHA enforcement case series 2011–2016: **38 investigations involving 66 heat-related illnesses**. "Many workers had predisposing medical conditions or used predisposing medications," comorbidities were more prevalent among those who died, **73% of fatal HRIs occurred during the worker's first week on the job**, and common heat-stroke findings were multi-organ failure, muscle breakdown and systemic inflammation. "Almost all HRIs occur when employers do not adhere to preventive guidelines." Evidence type: regulatory case series — [Tustin et al., J Occup Environ Med 2018; PMID 29851740](https://doi.org/10.1097/JOM.0000000000001365)

**Caffeine (controlled human data, modest effects, conflicting)**
- Meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials with ≥27 °C ambient (6 studies/52 participants for performance; 12 studies/205 for core temperature; typically 6 mg/kg 1 h pre-exercise, ~70 min exercise at 34 °C/47% RH): caffeine **non-significantly improved endurance performance by 2.1 ± 0.8%** and **significantly increased the rate of change in core temperature by 0.10 ± 0.03 °C/h (95% CI 0.02–0.19)** — described by the authors as a "trivial" thermal effect. Evidence type: systematic review with meta-analysis — [Naulleau et al., Sports Med 2022; PMID 35616851](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01692-1)
- A later crossover RCT (12 habituated males, 35 °C/40% RH, 5 mg/kg) found caffeine **raised pulmonary VO₂ 7.4%, heat production 7.9%, whole-body sweat rate 21% and evaporative heat transfer 16.5%, while decreasing estimated skin blood flow 14.1%** and raising core temperature 0.6%, with **no performance benefit** and reduced thermal comfort — i.e. a measurable thermogenic plus cutaneous-vasoconstriction penalty. Evidence type: double-blind randomized crossover trial — [John et al., Eur J Appl Physiol 2024; PMID 38568259](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-024-05460-z)
- Caffeine also **exacerbated hyperventilation and reductions in cerebral blood flow** in physically fit men exercising in the heat. Evidence type: human trial — [Med Sci Sports Exerc 2021; PMID 33044440](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002537)
- Energy drinks in youth, cardiovascular risk call to action. Evidence type: commentary/review — [Am J Med 2026; PMID 42556491](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2026.08.001)

### Inferences
- The military cohort's finding that **methylphenidate associated with mild but not severe heat illness** is a useful corrective: the stimulant signal is real but the catastrophic cases are dominated by **unregulated multi-ingredient sympathomimetic supplements**, where dose is unknown and ingredients are undeclared.
- The 1982 South African case (1 affected child out of 108 on the same hike, same ambient temperature) is arguably the single most persuasive case-level evidence in this entire literature that a drug — not the environment or fitness — was the determining variable.
- The Nelson cohort's month-2 peak and the OSHA series' first-week fatality pattern both point to **unacclimatized exposure as the dominant risk window**, which means drug risk should be weighted most heavily in the first weeks of a new job or training cycle rather than treated as a constant.

### Gaps
- **Pseudoephedrine**: no study found on pseudoephedrine and heat intolerance or exertional heat illness ("pseudoephedrine heat intolerance exercise" returned 0 results).
- **Creatine**: I found no study or case report linking creatine to heat illness. Searches for "creatine heat illness" returned general exertional-heat-illness papers with no creatine content. The frequently repeated concern about creatine and heat/cramping appears not to be supported by material I could verify here.
- **Diuretics for weight cutting** (wrestling, combat sports, weight-class athletes): no case report or study located specifically on diuretic-assisted weight cutting causing heat illness. Note the contrary epidemiologic signal: non-loop diuretics showed **no** heatwave interaction in the Japanese SCCS (PMID 42710533), and diuretics showed no core-temperature effect in the EClinicalMedicine meta-analysis (PMID 39513185).
- **Synephrine**: no source located.
- **SSRIs in exertional heat illness**: "SSRI hyperhidrosis heat illness" returned 0 results; the EClinicalMedicine meta-analysis found **antidepressants did not alter core temperature** under heat stress (PMID 39513185), which argues against a strong SSRI thermoregulatory effect in healthy young people.
- The ephedra sportsman death is sourced only to a BMJ news item; I did not locate a peer-reviewed case report or autopsy series for it. Treat as journalism.
- I did not access USARIEM technical reports or the Armed Forces Health Surveillance MSMR heat-illness annual reports directly. One adjacent MSMR item surfaced: exertional rhabdomyolysis among US active component service members 2021–2025 — [MSMR 2026; PMID 42308108] — which I did not read.

## Q4. Illicit/recreational drugs plus heat

### Takeaway
This is the strongest quantitative evidence in the whole topic: **cocaine-overdose mortality rises measurably with ambient temperature** in two independent New York datasets and one Quebec case-crossover, with no equivalent rise for opiate or other overdoses — a built-in negative control that makes the cocaine–heat interaction unusually credible. For MDMA, methamphetamine and synthetic cathinones the human evidence is case-level but the animal ambient-temperature experiments are consistent and mechanistically detailed.

### Cited Findings

**Cocaine — temperature-dependent mortality, with internal controls**
- New York City medical examiner data: a **threshold of 31.1 °C (88 °F)** maximum daily temperature was identified above which fatal cocaine overdoses rose steadily. On "hot days" the mean daily number of cocaine-overdose deaths was **2.34 vs. 1.76 — 33% higher (p<0.001)**. Crucially, **opiate overdoses (0.81 vs. 0.71, p=0.28), other drug overdoses (0.08 vs. 0.08, p=0.69), cocaine-positive homicides (18.9% vs. 19.5%) and cocaine-positive traffic fatalities (9.5% vs. 10.3%) showed no increase** — so the rise is not explained by more cocaine use in hot weather. Evidence type: retrospective medical-examiner study with 4 comparison groups — [Marzuk et al., JAMA 1998; PMID 9628710](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.279.22.1795)
- Extended NYC series 1990–2006 using generalized additive models: significant temperature–mortality relation **for all models where overdoses were due wholly or partly to cocaine (all p<0.05), but not for non-cocaine overdoses**; risk rose for weeks averaging **above 24 °C — substantially lower than prior estimates** — a threshold crossed in roughly 7 weeks per year in NYC. Evidence type: time-series/GAM analysis — [Bohnert et al., Addiction 2010; PMID 20219056](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2009.02887.x)
- Quebec case-crossover, May–September 2000–2013 (316 cocaine-overdose deaths, 446 other-drug deaths): compared with 20 °C, a **maximum weekly temperature of 30 °C gave OR 2.07 (95% CI 1.15–3.73) for cocaine-overdose death, vs. OR 1.03 (0.60–1.75) for other drug overdoses**; associations were present for maximum daily temperature on the day of and each of the 3 days preceding death. Evidence type: case-crossover (individual-level) — [Auger et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2017; PMID 28645059](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2017.04.019)

**MDMA**
- Human review: MDMA's thermal effect is dose-related — low doses little effect, moderate **+0.4 °C**, higher **+0.7 °C** mean increase in placebo-controlled lab studies, but **real-world dance-club studies found average increases over +1.0 °C** because of uncontrolled physical activity, ambient temperature and overcrowding. Mechanistically MDMA hits **both sides** of the heat-balance equation: cellular metabolic heat output rises *and* heat dissipation is stressed, **with the onset of sweating delayed**. Some users report heat reinstates MDMA's positive mood effects. "It is unclear why moderate hyperthermia can occasionally progress to severe hyperpyrexia." Evidence type: review of human studies — [Parrott, Drug Alcohol Depend 2012; PMID 21924843](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2011.08.012)
- Alcohol co-use as a risk multiplier: systematic review concluding alcohol negatively interacts with **most** drivers of MDMA hyperthermia — poikilothermia, high ambient temperature, vigorous dancing, vasoconstriction, dehydration, and **delayed initiation of sweating and diuresis**. Evidence type: systematic review — [van Amsterdam et al., Neurotox Res 2021; PMID 34554408](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12640-021-00416-z)
- The cooling-advice paradox: the rave-scene recommendation to **drink copiously to counteract hyperthermia** precipitates water intoxication; phenethylamine hyponatraemia arises from ADH secretion plus diaphoresis and polydipsia, with complications including cerebral oedema, cerebellar tonsillar herniation and coma, **particularly in women**. This is a genuine iatrogenic/harm-reduction tail case. Evidence type: review — [Faria et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2020; PMID 32460203](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108045)
- Extreme single outcome: acute MDMA toxicity leading to **fulminant hepatic failure and emergency liver transplantation**. Evidence type: single case report — [Cureus 2025; PMID 41561290](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.99716)

**Animal/mechanistic ambient-temperature work (directly answers "does heat worsen it?")**
- High ambient temperature **potentiated the toxicity and lethality of both MDMA and methcathinone** in mice; at room temperature even a very high methcathinone dose produced limited toxicity, which heat unmasked. Evidence type: animal experiment — [Chen et al., Pharmacol Biochem Behav 2020; PMID 32201298](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbb.2020.172912)
- Inhibiting the **dorsomedial hypothalamus** (but not medullary raphe pallidus) prevented mortality and attenuated hyperthermia and locomotion from MDMA at 32 °C; neither inhibition affected brown-adipose thermogenesis or tail blood flow, suggesting **locomotion itself is a key central contributor** to MDMA hyperthermia. Evidence type: animal mechanistic study — [Zaretsky et al., Pharmacol Res Perspect 2014; PMID 24765530](https://doi.org/10.1002/prp2.31)
- Brain hyperthermia from MDMA, methylone and MDPV is modulated by activity state and environment — [Curr Top Behav Neurosci 2017; PMID 27677782](https://doi.org/10.1007/7854_2016_35); social interaction plus warm ambient temperature amplify methylone/MDPV brain hyperthermia — [Neuropsychopharmacology 2015; PMID 25074640](https://doi.org/10.1038/npp.2014.191)
- **Dantrolene failed to reverse robust brain hyperthermia** induced by MDMA and methamphetamine in rats — a negative result relevant to proposed treatment. Evidence type: animal experiment — [Psychopharmacology 2023; PMID 36700960](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-023-06321-x)
- Clozapine **reverses** MDMA-induced hyperthermia and sympathetically mediated cutaneous vasoconstriction in rabbits and rats — [J Neurosci 2003; PMID 12867524](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.23-15-06385.2003); further pharmacological reversal strategies — [Neuropsychopharmacology 2016; PMID 26105141](https://doi.org/10.1038/npp.2015.182)
- Novel 5-HT2A agonist **25B-NBOMe produced hyperthermia only at 29 °C ambient, not at 23 °C**, via peripheral 5-HT2A-mediated vasoconstriction (tail temperature fell despite high ambient heat) plus brown-adipose thermogenesis driven by **decreased plasma serotonin**; suppressed by sarpogrelate but not by destruction of central noradrenaline or serotonin neurons. A clean demonstration of strictly ambient-temperature-dependent drug hyperthermia. Evidence type: animal mechanistic study — [Nakamura et al., J Pharmacol Exp Ther 2018; PMID 30206108](https://doi.org/10.1124/jpet.118.250217)
- Sex differences in cathinone thermal tolerance: with weekly methylone (10 mg/kg), **female rats developed tolerance by week 2 and a hypothermic response by week 3, while males remained hyperthermic through week 4** and achieved complete tolerance only at week 5; chronic methylone reduced UCP1 expression in female skeletal muscle and BAT, and UCP3 rose in both sexes. Evidence type: animal experiment — [Goldsmith et al., Temperature 2019; PMID 31934604](https://doi.org/10.1080/23328940.2019.1648988)
- Counterintuitive finding: several synthetic cathinones produced **hypothermia and reduced lethality** compared with methamphetamine and MDMA — cathinones are not uniformly more thermogenic. Evidence type: animal experiment — [Pharmacol Biochem Behav 2020; PMID 32061662](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbb.2020.172871)
- Hyperthermia **exacerbates methamphetamine cardiotoxicity** via neutrophil-dominant inflammation and Map3k6-associated p38/ERK signaling. Evidence type: animal mechanistic study — [Toxicol Appl Pharmacol 2026; PMID 42874877](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2026.118083)
- Synthetic cathinone fatality reviews describing hyperthermia as part of the lethal sympathomimetic toxidrome — [Int J Mol Sci 2023; PMID 37047201](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms24076230); [Eur Rev Med Pharmacol Sci 2018; PMID 29364498](https://doi.org/10.26355/eurrev_201801_14129); [Br J Clin Pharmacol 2020; PMID 31674690](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.14132)

### Inferences
- The cocaine studies are methodologically the best thing in this literature because the comparison groups (opiate overdoses, cocaine-positive homicides and traffic deaths) are internal controls that **rule out the obvious confounder of seasonal use patterns**. The two NYC studies disagree on threshold (31.1 °C daily max vs. 24 °C weekly average), which is a real and worth-noting discrepancy likely driven by the different exposure metric (daily maximum vs. weekly mean), not a contradiction.
- The 25B-NBOMe result (no hyperthermia at 23 °C, significant hyperthermia at 29 °C) and the MDMA/methcathinone potentiation result together make the strongest case in this whole research scope that **ambient temperature is not merely additive but permissive** — some drugs are not thermally dangerous at all until the environment is warm.
- MDMA's "delayed onset of sweating" is the key unifying detail: it places MDMA alongside the carbonic-anhydrase-inhibitor and anticholinergic drugs as a *dissipation* problem, not purely a thermogenesis problem, which explains why ambient conditions matter so much.

### Gaps
- I did not find a study quantifying **alcohol alone** and heat-related mortality; the alcohol evidence here is as an MDMA co-exposure.
- No specific case series located for **MDMA deaths at raves correlated with venue temperature** (search returned 0).
- The highest documented core temperatures in MDMA/cathinone fatalities were not captured; I did not retrieve individual autopsy temperature values.

## Q5. Other unusual thermoregulatory effects (cutaneous vasodilation, antiplatelets, CCBs, thyroid, dantrolene, anesthesia, hyperhidrosis/fluid loss, hypothermia paradoxes)

### Takeaway
Antiplatelet drugs are a genuinely non-obvious and well-studied cutaneous-vasodilation harm, with a notable discrepancy between two Penn State studies (effect on core temperature in middle-aged adults, no effect in older adults). The most underappreciated paradox is **antipsychotic-induced hypothermia**, which is potentially life-threatening and runs opposite to the usual heat narrative.

### Cited Findings

**Aspirin and clopidogrel — attenuated skin blood flow**
- Double-blind crossover in 14 healthy middle-aged (50–65 y) adults after 7 days of low-dose aspirin (81 mg/d) or clopidogrel (75 mg/d): after 40 min rest at 30 °C/40% RH, **esophageal temperature was significantly higher on both ASA and CLO vs. placebo (37.2 and 37.3 vs. 37.0 °C, both p<0.001)**, a difference that persisted through exercise; **mean body temperature thresholds for the onset of cutaneous vasodilation were shifted rightward for both drugs** (p<0.05). Evidence type: double-blind crossover trial — [Bruning et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2013; PMID 23135368](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e31827981dc)
- The same Penn State group in **older adults (>65 y, n=11)** under progressive heat stress (warm-humid 36 °C/52% RH and hot-dry 40 °C/21% RH, ~80 W·m⁻²): low-dose ASA **attenuated forearm blood flow and forearm vascular conductance (p≤0.04) but had no effect on core or skin temperature in either environment**. The authors note nearly 40% of US adults over 50 take aspirin. Evidence type: crossover trial — [Fisher et al., Physiol Rep 2025; PMID 40350968](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70375)
- Two further negative/attenuating studies: aspirin did not alter thermo-effector responses during mild whole-body passive heat stress in young men — [Int J Hyperthermia 2015; PMID 25697227](https://doi.org/10.3109/02656736.2014.999721); low-dose aspirin therapy effects on firefighter thermoregulation — [Saf Health Work 2015; PMID 26929836](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shaw.2015.06.003)

**Calcium channel blockers / antihypertensives**
- In the Japanese self-controlled case series, **calcium channel blockers and ACEi/ARBs** (but not non-loop diuretics) were associated with increased heat-related illness during heatwaves — IRR 1.64 (1.53–1.75) on antihypertensives vs. 1.42 (1.27–1.58) off, drug–temperature interaction p<0.01, with the authors' own caveat that confounding by indication is unresolved. Evidence type: self-controlled case series — [Harley et al., Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2026; PMID 42710533](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.70447)
- Countervailing experimental result: **no effect of stage 1 hypertension or antihypertensive medication on critical environmental limits** in the PSU HEAT Project. Evidence type: human trial — [Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 2025; PMID 40095927](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00038.2025)

**Beta-blockers, adrenaline, anti-Parkinson agents (quantified)**
- From the WHO-list meta-analysis: **non-selective beta-blockers +0.11 °C (95% CI 0.02–0.19), adrenaline +0.41 °C (0.21–0.61), anti-Parkinson's agents +0.13 °C (0.07–0.19)** core-temperature increase under heat stress; antidepressants, diuretics and weak anticholinergics showed no effect. The evidence base is almost entirely **healthy young men (16 of 353 participants were women; only one study enrolled a clinical population; mean age <30)** with short exposures — a major external-validity limitation. Evidence type: systematic review/meta-analysis — [Hospers et al., EClinicalMedicine 2024; PMID 39513185](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102886)

**Drug-induced hypothermia paradoxes**
- Case series of **5 patients with severe hypothermia after olanzapine and benperidol**; hypothermia risk appears highest in the first days after initiation or after a dose increase; **schizophrenic patients bear higher risk** than non-schizophrenic patients on antipsychotics (e.g. dementia, depression); antipsychotics with **strong 5-HT2 antagonism** are more frequently implicated. Evidence type: case series + literature review — [Kreuzer et al., J Clin Pharmacol 2012; PMID 21956608](https://doi.org/10.1177/0091270011409233)
- Supporting literature: hypothermia following antipsychotic drug use — [Eur J Clin Pharmacol 2007; PMID 17401555](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-007-0294-4); risk factors, frequency and diagnosis of atypical-antipsychotic hypothermia — [Int Clin Psychopharmacol 2019; PMID 30398998](https://doi.org/10.1097/YIC.0000000000000244); psychogeriatric inpatient characteristics — [Hum Psychopharmacol 2022; PMID 34532905](https://doi.org/10.1002/hup.2816)
- Note the clozapine mirror image: clozapine, chlorpromazine and risperidone **dose-dependently reduce emotional hyperthermia** — [Psychopharmacology 2017; PMID 28812124](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-017-4710-x) — and clozapine reverses MDMA hyperthermia in animals (PMID 12867524). So the same drug class sits on both sides of the thermoregulatory ledger.

**Clozapine fever — a diagnostic trap**
- Narrative review of 73 articles (2 meta-analyses, 14 reviews, 6 epidemiological studies, 11 clinical studies, 40 case reports): fever on clozapine initiation is **most often benign and transient**, but may be the first stage of agranulocytosis, NMS, myocarditis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, nephritis or colitis; separately, **heat stroke** is listed among the clozapine-associated adverse reactions that are **independent of duration of exposure** (along with pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, necrotizing colitis). Evidence type: narrative review — [Verdoux et al., Schizophr Res 2019; PMID 31378552](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.schres.2019.07.040)
- Illustrative differential-diagnosis case: distinguishing probable nitrofurantoin drug fever from antipsychotic-induced hyperthermia in a clozapine patient — [Ment Health Clin 2022; PMID 35801160](https://doi.org/10.9740/mhc.2022.06.205)
- Clozapine-treated patients' causes of death over 10 years in a catchment area. Evidence type: retrospective case-control — [Eur Neuropsychopharmacol 2020; PMID 32546415](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2020.05.011)

**Dantrolene and anesthesia**
- Dantrolene for heat stroke: not supported for routine adjuvant use; "administration in severe cases, or in which no improvement is observed, appears rational"; further trials needed — [Hadad et al., Crit Care 2005; PMID 15693989](https://doi.org/10.1186/cc2923). And the negative animal result for drug-induced brain hyperthermia (PMID 36700960).
- Perioperative management of coexisting mast cell activation syndrome and MH susceptibility — an example of stacked hyperthermia risks. Evidence type: case report — [Case Rep Anesthesiol 2026; PMID 42953382](https://doi.org/10.1155/cria/2750619)
- Dexmedetomidine attenuates methamphetamine-induced hyperthermia and hyperlocomotion in rats via anterior hypothalamic monoamine modulation. Evidence type: animal study — [Psychopharmacology 2026; PMID 42631872](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-026-07153-1)

**Histamine, sweating and hyperhidrosis**
- Histamine modulates sweating: histamine attenuates acetylcholine-induced sweating via GSK3β activation — [J Invest Dermatol 2014; PMID 23900020](https://doi.org/10.1038/jid.2013.323); histamine's role in sweating and atopic dermatitis — [Curr Probl Dermatol 2016; PMID 27584962](https://doi.org/10.1159/000446758); idiopathic pure sudomotor failure responding to oral antihistamine **with restoration of sweating** — [Acta Derm Venereol 2014; PMID 24535114](https://doi.org/10.2340/00015555-1820). This is a notable twist: in some sudomotor-failure phenotypes antihistamines *restore* rather than block sweating.

### Inferences
- The aspirin story is best read as **"attenuated skin blood flow is reproducible; whether it translates into a core-temperature penalty depends on age, environment and metabolic rate."** The 2013 middle-aged study found a core-temperature effect; the 2025 older-adult study found the same vascular attenuation *without* a core-temperature effect. Any report should present this as an unresolved dose–age–environment interaction, not a settled harm.
- The hypothermia literature is the strongest corrective to a one-directional "drugs make you overheat" narrative: the *same* 5-HT2-antagonist property that protects against MDMA hyperthermia creates hypothermia risk on initiation and dose escalation. Thermoregulatory drug harm is bidirectional.
- The meta-analysis's demographic limitation (healthy young men, <30, short exposures, one clinical population, 4.5% women) means the quantified core-temperature effects are almost certainly **underestimates for the populations that actually die in heat waves** — frail elderly, polypharmacy, chronic disease. That mismatch between where the controlled evidence exists and where the deaths occur is itself a headline finding.

### Gaps
- **Thyroid hormone excess / thyroid storm and heat exposure**: I found **no** case report or study ("thyroid storm heat exposure case report" and "levothyroxine thyrotoxicosis heat stroke" both returned 0; "thyroid hormone heat intolerance thermogenesis overdose" returned 0). The only adjacent item is a thyroid-storm differential-diagnosis review — [Am J Emerg Med 2023; PMID 37104908](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2023.03.035) — which I did not read for heat content. Heat intolerance in thyrotoxicosis is textbook physiology, but I could not verify a drug-induced-thyrotoxicosis-plus-ambient-heat harm case.
- **Opioid withdrawal diaphoresis and thermoregulation**: 0 results. The fluid-loss-via-withdrawal-sweating pathway appears undocumented in indexed literature.
- **SSRI-induced hyperhidrosis as a heat/fluid-loss risk**: 0 results for the combined query, and the meta-analysis found no antidepressant core-temperature effect. I could not substantiate this mechanism.
- I did not verify FDA label language for any drug in this report, nor retrieve FAERS disproportionality statistics beyond the zonisamide pediatric reporting rates in PMID 12770670. Any label-based claim in the final report should be independently checked.
- A useful-looking general review I did not read in full but which the report-writer may want: Pediatric/general drug-heat scoping review for older adults — [Pharmacy (Basel) 2026; PMID 42200961](https://doi.org/10.3390/pharmacy14030074), which screened 61 primary studies plus 41 grey-literature sources and names diuretics, ACEi, ARBs, anticholinergics and antipsychotics as the classes most frequently associated with heat-related illness, while concluding that **"specific, evidence-based guidance on managing drug-heat interactions is limited"** and that few experimental studies evaluate medication's specific role.
