# Rare and Extreme Heat Harms to Medications, Devices and Supply Chains (Outside the Body)

Verification note: PMIDs and DOIs below marked [verified] were pulled directly from PubMed E-utilities records (efetch/abstract) or PubMed search result listings during this session. Items marked [abstract-only] mean I read the abstract but not the full text. Items marked [unverified ID] have no confirmed PMID/DOI. I fabricated no identifiers.

## 1. Stability of drugs at extreme temperatures (insulin, epinephrine, EMS kits, oxytocin, others)

### Takeaway
The evidence is uneven. Insulin in in-use pens holds up much better than labels suggest at a steady 37°C, and so do midazolam, ketamine, diazepam and epinephrine 1 mg/mL. The heat-fragile tail is lorazepam, succinylcholine, dilute epinephrine (1:10,000 / 0.1 mg/mL), atropine and naloxone under bench heat, cefazolin at 50°C, lyophilized tenecteplase, epinephrine auto-injectors under prolonged 70°C, and oxytocin. The most alarming field result is from South African ambulances: adrenaline lost about 25% and ketamine more than 30% within 4 weeks. It contradicts the US ketamine data.

### Cited Findings

**Epinephrine / auto-injectors**
- Lab: 25 in-date epinephrine auto-injectors held at a constant 70°C for 10 days delivered 77.7 ± 3.3% of the labeled dose (controls 103.7%). 5 days constant gave 96.8%, and 5 days cyclic 70°C gave 87.2%. Evidence type: lab stability study. Rachid O et al., Drug Dev Ind Pharm 2016. PMID 25997362, DOI 10.3109/03639045.2015.1035283 [verified, abstract-only] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25997362/)
- Lab plus rat bioassay: epinephrine 1:10,000 heated to 70°C for 8 h/day degraded significantly by 8 weeks and was 64% degraded by 12 weeks. Pressor potency fell about 30% in rats. 1:1,000 epinephrine was not significantly degraded even at 12 weeks, and cold had no effect. Dilute "push-dose"/cardiac-arrest concentrations are therefore the fragile ones. Grant TA et al., Am J Emerg Med 1994. PMID 8179739, DOI 10.1016/0735-6757(94)90148-1 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8179739/)
- Systematic review (9 studies): heat degraded epinephrine only with prolonged exposure, and constant heat did more damage than cycling. Real-world fluctuations and cold caused no significant degradation. All 40 auto-injectors tested fired. Only 2 small studies involved auto-injectors. Parish HG et al., Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol 2016. PMID 27221065, DOI 10.1016/j.anai.2016.04.006 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27221065/)
- Device-mechanical failure, an extreme case: in a Seattle-led study, auto-injectors heated to about 80–89°C for 8 hours and then cooled became hard to remove from their carrier tubes in 84% of cases. A Polish pilot found that a few hours in a parked car on a sunny summer day lowered epinephrine concentration with no visible change, while the devices stayed functional. Secondary reporting only. [unverified ID] — [Allergic Living](https://www.allergicliving.com/2020/03/11/if-your-auto-injector-gets-hot-it-may-fail/); [SnackSafely](https://snacksafely.com/2018/12/study-single-exposure-to-the-heat-of-a-car-can-lower-effective-dose-of-epinephrine-auto-injector)
- Field: in LA County ALS units (desert, marine and helicopter), atropine, epinephrine and lidocaine prefilled syringes showed no degradation over 45 days. This held even though 7 sites exceeded 40°C, for up to 795 minutes, and the highest MKT was 28.9°C. Gill MA et al., Am J Health Syst Pharm 2004. PMID 15061431, DOI 10.1093/ajhp/61.6.597 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15061431/)
- Epinephrine prefilled syringe stability/sterility: Kerddonfak S et al., Asian Pac J Allergy Immunol 2010. PMID 20527517 [PMID seen in PubMed listing; results not read] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20527517/)

**EMS drug kits (benzodiazepines, succinylcholine, ketamine, others)**
- Field, 2 southwestern US EMS systems, May–Aug 2011, mean MKT 31.6°C. Lorazepam fell to 95.6% at 60 d, 90.3% at 90 d and 86.5% at 120 d. It was the only drug below 90%. Midazolam held at 99.0% and diazepam at 97.0% at 120 d. McMullan JT et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2014. PMID 24548058, DOI 10.3109/10903127.2013.869642 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24548058/)
- Field, 14 US metro EMS systems over 60 days in spring–summer. Midazolam showed no temperature dependence. Lorazepam showed small but significant degradation correlated with mean kinetic temperature (adjusted R² 0.278). McMullan JT et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2013. PMID 23148574, DOI 10.3109/10903127.2012.722177 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23148574/)
- Real-world, Belgium, 1 year in an EMS physician vehicle against a 90% threshold. Lorazepam was unstable within 4 weeks in both the vehicle and room-temperature arms. Succinylcholine was stable only about 1 month in the vehicle (2 months at room temperature). Adrenaline and methylergonovine were stable for 1 year. De Winter S et al., Ann Emerg Med 2013. PMID 23711878, DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2013.04.018 [verified, abstract-only] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23711878/)
- Real-world, emergency medical car over 1 year (13.9–33.9°C, median 22.8°C). Succinylcholine fell to 89% at 12 months and was the only failure. Amiodarone, rocuronium, fentanyl and epinephrine stayed at 96.3–103%. Welter C et al., Ann Emerg Med 2022. PMID 35717271, DOI 10.1016/j.annemergmed.2022.05.001 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35717271/)
- Mass spectrometry: succinylcholine already contained breakdown fragments at day 0 and reached 10% degradation at about 90 days in ambulances (cabin 56–89°F). Temperature variation did not significantly contribute. Merlin MA et al., Acad Emerg Med 2010. PMID 20624143, DOI 10.1111/j.1553-2712.2010.00766.x [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20624143/)
- Extreme field result from South Africa: 4 EMS bases, ambient 18.7–44°C, 8 weeks. Adrenaline content fell 24.9% at 4 weeks and 22.7% at 8 weeks. Ketamine lost more than 30% by 4 weeks. Both failed the 20% threshold. Caveat: control concentrations of etomidate and rocuronium differed from the labeled strength (for example rocuronium about 6.5 mg/mL vs 10 mg/mL labeled), which raises analytical-method concerns. Sobuwa S et al., Afr J Emerg Med 2024. PMID 38173689, DOI 10.1016/j.afjem.2023.11.009 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38173689/)
- Contradicting that ketamine result: 120 vials of ketamine 50 mg/mL over 6 months in a real EMS unit and in chambers at 120°F, 107°F, 96.5°F and 70°F showed no significant degradation. The lowest median was 96.4% of label. Foertsch MJ et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2022. PMID 34028315, DOI 10.1080/10903127.2021.1934203 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34028315/)
- Bench test of a National Park Service kit at constant 45°C, −20°C or alternating, for 4 weeks. Atropine was undetectable after 4 weeks of heat. Naloxone degraded in a heat-dependent way. Midazolam was at 51–56%, but in all arms including controls, so the loss was not heat-dependent; this conflicts with the McMullan field data. Hydromorphone, morphine and ondansetron stayed above 90%. Armenian P et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2017. PMID 28059581, DOI 10.1080/10903127.2016.1258098 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28059581/)
- Older simulation of 23 prehospital drugs under extreme environments for up to 4 weeks. Isoproterenol lost 11%. Epinephrine changed ionization state, with no percentage given. The other 21 showed no breakdown products. Valenzuela TD et al., Ann Emerg Med 1989. PMID 2916782, DOI 10.1016/s0196-0644(89)80109-x [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2916782/)
- Thrombolytic: lyophilized tenecteplase 50 mg held at 44.9°C for only 8 h before reconstitution had lower solubility and lost monomer and single-chain protein. Clot lysis was also reduced (lower D-dimer generation). 4°C preserved bioactivity. Henkel E et al., Prehosp Disaster Med 2020. PMID 32686630, DOI 10.1017/S1049023X20000928 [verified, abstract-only] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32686630/)
- Tranexamic acid showed no significant degradation after 7 days of heating cycles (P = .722). Loner C et al., Prehosp Disaster Med 2019. PMID 31043187, DOI 10.1017/S1049023X19004308 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31043187/)
- Military temperature extremes, with n = 1 sample per condition (very weak evidence). Cefazolin fell to 87.7% after 14 days at 50°C and also failed on pH. 9 of 10 drugs retained at least 90%, including ceftriaxone, benzylpenicillin, hydrocortisone, amiodarone and TXA. Shah S et al., BMJ Mil Health 2026. PMID 42580819, DOI 10.1136/military-2026-003387 [verified via PubMed record] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42580819/)
- Cold-side physical change in the same literature: propofol formed lipid droplets after 12 h at 0.6 to −3.4°C and at −22°C. Moeckel J et al., Air Med J 2025. PMID 39993848, DOI 10.1016/j.amj.2024.10.007 [verified]
- Also in PubMed (not read): Gottwald MD, "Prehospital stability of diazepam and lorazepam," Am J Emerg Med 1999, PMID 10452426 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10452426/)

**Ambient extremes in EMS vehicles (exposure data)**
- Qatar rapid-response cars logged for 12 months at 10-minute intervals. Peaks reached 65.7°C inside the car and 59.1°C inside medication bags. Mean kinetic temperature exceeded USP's 30°C limit and sometimes exceeded 50°C. Alaghawani NA et al., BMC Emerg Med 2025. PMID 40596924, DOI 10.1186/s12873-025-01255-3 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40596924/)
- Helicopter EMS drug boxes were outside USP 15–30°C on 56% of summer days and 62% of winter nights. Madden JF et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 1999. PMID 9921737, DOI 10.1080/10903129908958902 [verified]

**Insulin**
- Randomized crossover trial: glargine pens stored at 37°C for 21 days gave the same CGM glucose as refrigerated pens (158.7 vs 157.0 mg/dL, p = 0.72; n = 40). UHPLC content was also equal (p = 0.97). Kongmalai T et al., BMJ Open Diabetes Res Care 2022. PMID 36585035, DOI 10.1136/bmjdrc-2022-003105 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36585035/)
- In-use pens of 4 basal insulins kept 28 days at 29.7°C, 37°C or cycling held 97–101.5% of control content. Kongmalai T et al., Exp Clin Endocrinol Diabetes 2021. PMID 31634960, DOI 10.1055/a-1010-5466 [verified]
- Refugee camp: Dagahaley camp in Kenya was logged oscillating between 25 and 37°C. Insulins exposed to that cycle in the lab for 4 weeks met pharmacopeial standards, and receptor/Akt bioactivity matched 2–8°C controls. Kaufmann B et al., PLoS One 2021. PMID 33534816, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0245372 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33534816/)
- Contrasting older animal data: rabbits given regular or biphasic human insulin stored at 32°C or 37°C for 28 days had no significant glucose fall compared with insulin stored at 5°C. The authors concluded potency was lost. Vimalavathini R, Gitanjali B, Indian J Med Res 2009. PMID 19797814 [PMID from search-result URL; abstract not read in full] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19797814/)
- Low-tech cooling: traditional clay pots for insulin storage in hot climates. Gill G, Trop Doct 2002. PMID 12405311, DOI 10.1177/004947550203200421 [verified ID; results not available]
- Hajj: daytime temperatures run 37–45°C. A Makkah survey found 62.3% of pilgrims with diabetes had no storage facility for insulin, and there were no mobile coolers for travel between holy sites. Survey only; no measured insulin degradation. [unverified ID] — [JPMA archive](https://archive.jpma.org.pk/article-details/9203); [PMC4953310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4953310)

**Oxytocin / carbetocin (maternal health)**
- Field simulation in rural Ghana: unrefrigerated Oxytocin-in-Uniject could be kept 30–40 days at typical field conditions with less than 10% wastage, judged by time-temperature indicators. [abstract-only; PMID not confirmed] — [GHSP 2014;2(3):285](https://www.ghspjournal.org/content/2/3/285)
- 74% of oxytocin samples bought at private pharmacies in Ghana were outside specification for active ingredient. A systematic review found a high prevalence of poor-quality oxytocin in LMICs. WHO prequalification attributes out-of-specification field samples to storage outside refrigeration. Clinical consequence: postpartum haemorrhage treatment failure (inferred). [secondary] — [PMC4168628](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4168628); [WHO PQ notice](https://extranet.who.int/prequal/sites/default/files/document_files/150%20stability%20data%20Oxytocin%20Oct2018.pdf)
- SRA-approved or WHO-prequalified oxytocin labeled for 2–8°C passed stability testing. [abstract-only] — [PMC7646793](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7646793); heat-stable carbetocin is the tested alternative — [PMC6001700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6001700)

### Inferences
- Concentration matters: the 0.1 mg/mL epinephrine used in cardiac arrest and push-dose kits is the vulnerable form, not the 1 mg/mL ampoule.
- "Heat-fragile" in EMS evidence mostly means lorazepam and succinylcholine, which are labeled for refrigeration. Midazolam and ketamine are robust in most US studies. The South African and NPS bench outliers may reflect method differences or genuinely hotter exposure.
- Insulin's in-use 4-week tolerance up to roughly 37°C is now supported by an RCT. The real tail risk is exposure above 37°C (cars, direct sun, pump-on-body charging), where no clinical-outcome studies exist.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed measurements for GLP-1 pens (semaglutide, liraglutide), glucagon kits, levothyroxine, nitroglycerin, ophthalmic drops, monoclonal antibodies or vaccines in heat. A PubMed query covering these failed because of a page-size error, and I did not rerun it within the tool budget. Nitroglycerin volatilization into plastic and levothyroxine humidity sensitivity are well known, but I have no verified citations for them in these notes.
- No auto-injector field study in EMS vehicles exists, and the 80–89°C carrier-tube study lacks a verified PMID.
- Carbetocin heat-stable data is cited only via a PMC link that I did not read.

## 2. Toxic degradation products (drugs that become harmful, not just weaker)

### Takeaway
The classic and best-documented case is reversible Fanconi syndrome from outdated or degraded tetracycline. It was described in 1960s–70s case reports, and anhydro-4-epitetracycline was identified as the nephrotoxin in animal studies. Heat, moisture and low pH drive its formation. I found no other well-documented case of a heat-degraded drug causing toxicity in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Case report: acquired Fanconi syndrome after degraded tetracycline. Cleveland WW et al., J Pediatr 1965. PMID 14258922 [PMID from PubMed listing; no abstract] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14258922/)
- Case series/report from Thailand, a hot and humid climate: Varavithya W et al., J Med Assoc Thai 1971. PMID 5547594 [PMID from PubMed listing; no abstract] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5547594/)
- Clinical picture: nausea, vomiting, proteinuria, acidosis, glycosuria and aminoaciduria after expired tetracycline, with recovery in about 1 month. The epi-anhydro and anhydro degradation products were the suspected cause. — [Scilit: "Reversible 'Fanconi Syndrome' Caused by Degraded Tetracycline"](https://scilit.com/publications/44a85de3a2e1706f83f4b7a201152c45) [JAMA 1963 per secondary sources; ID unverified]
- Animal: of 3 degradation products tested in rats and dogs, only anhydro-4-epitetracycline reproduced the urinary abnormalities. Benitz & Diermeier, Proc Soc Exp Biol Med 1964, DOI 10.3181/00379727-115-29082 (DOI taken from the journal URL). — [EBM journal PDF](https://www.ebm-journal.org/journals/experimental-biology-and-medicine/articles/10.3181/00379727-115-29082/pdf)
- Mechanism support: a 1981 Japanese study found 4-epi-tetracycline damaged the renal brush-border membrane in rabbits. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jscc1971a/20/0/20_45/_article/-char/ja)
- Related heat-generated fragments: succinylcholine vials already contain breakdown fragments at day 0, and those fragments increase in ambulances (PMID 20624143, above). No toxicity from the fragments was shown.

### Inferences
- Modern tetracycline formulations were reformulated to reduce this risk, so the hazard is now mostly historical. It may still matter for substandard or old stock in hot, humid settings. This is an inference, not documented in these sources.

### Gaps
- My search did not confirm other candidates: paraldehyde decomposing to acetic acid or acetaldehyde, carbamazepine tablets hardening in humidity and leading to seizures, aspirin hydrolysis to salicylic acid, and degraded epinephrine forming adrenochrome. The PubMed query returned nothing usable. They need targeted follow-up before anyone cites them.

## 3. Physical changes: inhalers, suppositories, patches, softgels

### Takeaway
Pressurized MDIs deliver more drug, or finer particles, when warm and less when cold. Storing albuterol HFA in a car trunk in Tucson for six months did not significantly change dose delivery once the canister was tested at normal temperature. A real-world logger study suggests a smaller lung dose in hot summers.

### Cited Findings
- Proventil HFA and Ventolin HFA were stored in a vehicle trunk in Tucson, AZ, for 6 months. Delivery was not significantly altered after extreme storage, but delivery did change appreciably when tested at temperatures outside the recommended range. Hoye WL et al. (University of Arizona) [ID unverified] — [UA experts page](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/effects-of-extreme-temperatures-on-drug-delivery-of-albuterol-sul/)
- Patients carried salbutamol inhalers with temperature and humidity loggers for 2–3 weeks. The authors projected smaller lung doses in hot summer than from well-kept inhalers. [result magnitude not verified] — [Aerosol Sci Technol, DOI 10.1080/02786826.2016.1240354 per URL](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02786826.2016.1240354)
- Cold metaproterenol canisters released considerably less drug, attributed to vapor pressure that rises with temperature ("Effect of Canister Temperature on Performance of Metered-Dose Inhalers," 1991). Separately, beclomethasone aerosol particle size fell and respirable fraction rose with temperature. — [Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/59a2163f7aec3594b7934a62ce448374); [Drug Dev Ind Pharm, DOI 10.3109/03639049809089947 per URL](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.3109/03639049809089947)

### Gaps
- I found no verified studies in this session on suppository melting, cream or softgel failure, DPI humidity clumping, nasal sprays, or sweat-induced loss of transdermal patch adhesion. Only consumer guidance exists for CGM adhesion (next section).

## 4. Devices: insulin pumps, CGMs, glucometers, power-dependent equipment

### Takeaway
There is a case report of DKA after an insulin pump was exposed to heat and sunlight (Pryce 2009, German). Bench data show pump reservoirs can exceed insulin's 37°C limit during charging or ambient heat. Glucose meters and test strips give errors of 20–30% after short heat and humidity shocks, and the direction depends on the enzyme chemistry.

### Cited Findings
- Case report: "Diabetic ketoacidosis by exposure of insulin pump to heat and sunlight." Pryce R, Praxis (Bern) 2009. PMID 19424950, DOI 10.1024/1661-8157.98.10.543 [verified ID; German, no abstract, details unknown] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19424950/)
- Pump temperature rose 6.4°C during a long lithium-ion charge and 4.4°C in a 35°C chamber, pushing it above the 37°C insulin limit. Industry-sponsored (Animas). Vereshchetin P et al., Med Devices (Auckl) 2016. PMID 27789976, DOI 10.2147/MDER.S116666 [verified]. Tandem disputed the clinical relevance in a reply ("Reported insulin pump temperature fluctuations lack clinical relevance") — [Dovepress](https://dovepress.com/reported-insulin-pump-temperature-fluctuations-lack-clinical-relevance-peer-reviewed-article-MDER)
- In normal wear, reservoir temperature ranged 25–37°C, averaging about 30°C, and was higher than ambient because of body heat. 3–5 volunteers per season; BD authors. Herr JK et al., J Diabetes Sci Technol 2014. PMID 24876429, DOI 10.1177/1932296814532327 [verified]
- Lispro in a MiniMed pump was chemically stable for 14 days at 37°C. Sharrow SD et al., Diabetes Technol Ther 2012. PMID 22107252 [PMID from PubMed listing]. Aspart at 37 ± 2°C for up to 7 days: Senstius J, Diabetes Technol Ther 2007, PMID 17316101 [listing]. Lispro in infusion systems over 48 h: Lougheed WD, Diabetes Care 1997, PMID 9203437 [listing]
- Glucose meters and strips: about 15 minutes at roughly 42°C and 83% RH produced meter bias up to 20 mg/dL and combined error of about 30%. Louie RF et al., J Diabetes Sci Technol 2014 [PMID likely 24876542 per CNGB record URL; not confirmed on PubMed] — [CNGB](https://db.cngb.org/data_resources/literature/24876542)
- Strips heat-stressed at 40°C for up to 4 weeks gave elevated results. — [PMC2717087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717087)
- In a tropical Bangkok hospital, high RH lowered readings on a glucose-oxidase system, the opposite direction to the heat-stress studies. — [PMC5032940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5032940)
- 8 meter systems tested for 50 days were unreliable even within manufacturer temperature and humidity limits, and temperature mattered more than humidity. Diabetes Technol Ther 2006, DOI 10.1089/dia.2006.0051 (DOI from URL) [abstract via search only]
- Under a 24-h temperature and humidity cycle modeled on post-Katrina conditions, meter errors reached about 22–24%. Louie et al., Disaster Med Public Health Prep [ID unverified] — [Cambridge PDF](https://www-cambridge-org.ezproxyberklee.flo.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/05A4AF744FB90B40601E8376BAB4CC95/S1935789300004444a.pdf/effects-of-dynamic-temperature-and-humidity-stresses-on-point-of-care-glucose-testing-for-disaster-care.pdf)
- CGM adhesion: the only peer-reviewed source is "Skin and adhesive issues with CGMs: a sticky situation" (J Diabetes Sci Technol 2014, PMID 24876416), which does not quantify heat or sweat effects. Claims that heat halves wear time are unsourced. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24876416/)

### Gaps
- I found no verified data on implanted devices (pacemakers, ICDs, neurostimulators) overheating, CPAP or nebulizer failure in heat, oxygen concentrator thermal shutdown, home dialysis machines, portable ventilators, or loss of transdermal patch adhesion in sweat.
- Apart from Pryce 2009, I found no peer-reviewed DKA or hyperglycemia case series from heat-degraded pump insulin.

## 5. Mail-order and supply-chain temperature excursions

### Takeaway
Logger studies show mail-order packages spend most of transit outside USP controlled room temperature (68–77°F) in every season and with every carrier.

### Cited Findings
- 36 packages, 3 carriers, 2 shipping methods, 2 seasons (NJ/CA/TN). Packages spent an average of 68.3% of transit time out of range. In summer, 3-day packages were out of range 43.1% of the time vs 13.6% for next-business-day. Chowdhury DA et al., J Am Pharm Assoc 2023;63(3):847-852. DOI 10.1016/j.japh.2023.02.002 [DOI via OUCI; PMID likely 36858884 per library record, not confirmed on PubMed]. Note: this is JAPhA, not AJHP. — [OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4VZnxvW4); [record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/cmedm__36858884)
- University of Utah: 48 non-refrigerated logger packages shipped to 6 US locations in winter 2019–20 and summer 2020. Every shipment went outside 68–77°F at some point. Conference presentation (ASHP Midyear 2020), not peer-reviewed. — [Pharmacy Times](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/study-shows-mail-order-prescriptions-may-be-often-exposed-to-unsafe-temperatures)
- Simulated insulin transit: regular, NPH and 70/30 insulin shipped 24–120 h in summer and winter profiles, in noninsulated or gel-pack mailers, all met USP potency. Chandler C et al., Am J Health Syst Pharm 2008. PMID 18463345, DOI 10.2146/ajhp070347 [verified]
- Vendor case study (promotional, low quality): after a Florida mail-order pharmacy added indicators, temperature-related returns fell from about 15 per week to 15 per quarter. — [SpotSee](https://spotsee.io/case-study/mail-order-pharmacy-case-study-warmmark-qr/)

### Inferences
- Exposure is very common, but the measured consequence for robust products like insulin is small. The harm is likely concentrated in heat-fragile products (biologics, suspensions, lorazepam-like molecules) and in prolonged summer ground shipping.

### Gaps
- There are no outcome studies linking mailbox or porch heat to clinical failure. I did not identify mailbox-specific temperature studies.

## 6. Power outages and heat waves: medication spoilage and device failure

### Takeaway
Outages amplify heat harms. A modeling study projects that heat-wave mortality would more than double during a blackout. Observed data show emergency demand surging from failed home respiratory equipment (NYC area 2003), from dialysis and medication refills (Houston, Beryl 2024), and higher nursing-home mortality without power (Irma 2017). I found no verified Pacific Northwest 2021 heat dome studies on medication or devices.

### Cited Findings
- 18-hour blackout in August 2003 (urban EMS): call volume rose about 250% in the first 10 hours. 37% of calls (n = 31) were respiratory, and 52% of those (n = 16) involved failed home respiratory equipment. 16% were heat-related, and on-scene time rose 63%. Rand DA et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2005. PMID 16263671, DOI 10.1080/10903120500255909 [verified] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263671/)
- Modeling a multiday grid failure during a heat wave in Atlanta, Detroit and Phoenix: heat mortality more than doubled in all 3 cities. About 50% of Phoenix residents would need emergency care (3% in Atlanta). Stone B Jr et al., Environ Sci Technol 2023. PMID 37219950, DOI 10.1021/acs.est.2c09588 [verified]
- Victoria, Australia, 2012–2016, 2.02 million ambulance attendances: heat-related ambulance risk nearly doubled during outages, about 1,110 extra cases. Xu Z et al., Environ Res 2025. PMID 40692025, DOI 10.1016/j.envres.2025.122396 [verified]
- After Hurricane Irma, Florida nursing homes that lost power had higher 7-day mortality (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.05–1.48) and 30-day mortality (OR 1.12). Skarha J et al., JAMA Health Forum 2021. PMID 35977265, DOI 10.1001/jamahealthforum.2021.3900 [verified]
- Hurricane Beryl in Houston (2024, summer, about 50% of the city without power): ED visits rose significantly for dialysis (days 0–3), medication refills (days 1–9), heat illness and CO poisoning. Caton K et al., Disaster Med Public Health Prep 2025. PMID 40545799, DOI 10.1017/dmp.2025.10086 [verified]
- Hurricane Maria in Puerto Rico (qualitative, 40 interviews): lengthy outages prevented refrigerated medication storage and equipment use, and participants described deaths among NCD patients.
