# Heat exposes the weak points in everyday prescriptions

Heat raises risk for patients on common prescriptions through four main routes. Some drugs block the body's cooling (anticholinergics, topiramate and zonisamide, antipsychotics). Some deepen dehydration and kidney stress (diuretics, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, and lithium and other renally cleared drugs). Some change how much drug gets in (fentanyl and other patches, insulin). And heat can damage the drug or device itself (epinephrine, glucose strips, insulin pumps, power-dependent equipment). The evidence varies widely in strength. The best population studies find only moderate risk increases for antipsychotics, anticholinergics and loop diuretics, with rate or odds ratios mostly between 1.2 and 2.8. The most careful recent designs, which compare each patient with themselves, find that the drugs mostly mark patients who are already vulnerable rather than multiplying the effect of a heat wave. Most of the dramatic harms rest on single case reports: fatal heat stroke on topiramate at normal doses, fatal fentanyl-patch overdose in a sauna, and recurrent summer heat stroke on oxybutynin. That makes them real but impossible to rank by frequency. The prescribers involved cover nearly every specialty, including primary care, cardiology, psychiatry, neurology, pediatrics, endocrinology, pain and palliative care, urology, transplant, and EMS medical direction. Official guidance is mostly built from mechanism-based drug lists dating to the 2003 French heat wave, with no formal evidence grading. None of the eight guidance sources reviewed mentions heat and fentanyl patches or SGLT2 inhibitors. Only France addresses glucose-meter errors in heat, and only the US documents address power outages and photosensitivity. For a visual evidence map, the most useful single idea is that the harms guidance emphasizes most and the harms with the strongest evidence overlap only partly. Several of the gravest rare harms have no guidance at all.

## Most drug-heat evidence is case-level, and strong designs mostly find no added heat-wave effect

A non-clinician building an evidence map first needs to know what "evidence" means here, because the topic mixes very different kinds of proof. This report uses six tiers. At the top are **randomized trials and meta-analyses**, which in this field mostly means short laboratory heat-stress experiments. Next come **cohort, case-control, case-crossover and self-controlled case series** studies of real populations. Below those are **case series** (several patients described together), **single case reports**, **lab or bench studies** of drugs and devices without patients, and finally **guidance or expert opinion only**. The table below shows what each tier can and cannot tell a heat-wave planner.

| Tier | What it shows | Typical example in this topic | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| RCT / meta-analysis | Whether a drug changes body temperature or sweating under controlled heat | Diphenhydramine crossover trial; 2024 meta-analysis of 35 heat-stress trials | Participants are mostly healthy young men in short exposures |
| Cohort / case-crossover / SCCS | How often drug users are hospitalized or die in heat | US Medicare 2020, Japan 2024 and 2026, French 2003 case-control studies | Can't separate the drug from the disease it treats |
| Case series | A pattern across several patients | Topiramate hypohidrosis in children; 2003 Paris ED mortality | No comparison group |
| Case report | That a harm *can* happen | Fatal topiramate heat stroke; fentanyl sauna death | Can't estimate frequency |
| Lab / bench | Drug or device degradation, absorption mechanisms | Epinephrine auto-injectors at 70°C; glucose strips in heat | May not reflect real patients |
| Guidance only | What agencies recommend | CDC, NYC, UKHSA drug lists | Usually ungraded, often mechanism-based |

The best population studies point in a consistent direction. A US Medicare self-controlled case series of 9,721 older adults with chronic disease found that antipsychotics (**RR 1.37**) and anticholinergics (**RR 1.16**) raised heat-related hospitalization across the whole summer. Loop diuretics raised it in patients with dementia (1.54), heart failure (1.64) and prior heart attack (1.84). Beta-blockers and stimulants showed no association, and the authors found **"no meaningful synergistic interactions"** between heat waves and any medication ([Layton et al., PLoS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0243665)). A Japanese self-controlled case series from 2024 reached a similar conclusion for psychiatry. Heat-wave risk was nearly the same whether patients with severe mental illness were on antipsychotics or not (IRR 1.48 vs 1.45, interaction p=0.53), and the same held for depression and antidepressants. The risk sat in the condition, not the prescription ([Wong et al., Psychol Med 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11769904/)).

The older French 2003 case-control studies compare different people rather than following the same person over time, and they produced the large numbers that guidance still cites. Among 56 heat-related ED admissions in Bordeaux, anticholinergics carried **OR 6.0**, antipsychotics 4.6 and anxiolytics 2.4 ([Martin-Latry et al., Eur Psychiatry 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17513091/)). A national French mortality study found antipsychotics aOR 2.09 and antidepressants 1.71 ([NIEHS summary of Nordon et al. 2009](https://tools.niehs.nih.gov/cchhl/index.cfm/detail/1508)). The Nordon figures come from a secondary summary, and no PMID or DOI could be verified. The small Bordeaux sample and its controls, who were simply people holding a July 2003 prescription, probably inflate those odds ratios.

Physiology trials support the cautious reading. A 2024 meta-analysis of 35 heat-stress trials (353 participants) found that antidepressants, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, diuretics and weak anticholinergics did not change core temperature. Strongly anticholinergic drugs raised it by about 0.4°C, but that evidence is "essentially limited to atropine." Only 16 of the 353 participants were women, the mean age was under 30, and only one study enrolled patients ([Hospers et al., eClinicalMedicine 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102886)). This is the key structural problem in the field. **Controlled evidence comes from young, healthy men, while heat-wave deaths happen in frail, older, multi-medicated people.** The null trial results are therefore probably underestimates for the people at real risk, and they cannot rule harm out.

Two conflicts belong on any honest map. First, in the national French data, **anxiolytics and hypnotics appeared protective for death (aOR 0.85)** while they appeared harmful for hospitalization in Bordeaux, and they were null in Australia ([NIEHS summary](https://tools.niehs.nih.gov/cchhl/index.cfm/detail/1508); [MedicalXpress on Kalisch Ellett 2016](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2016-07-meds-hospitalization-dehydration-heat-linked-illness.amp)). That pattern almost certainly reflects healthier users rather than a real benefit. Second, the 1999 Cincinnati heat-wave investigation found mental illness carried OR 14.0 for heat death. Toxicology suggested decedents **may not have been taking their psychotropics**, and a working air conditioner was by far the strongest protection (OR 0.03) ([Kaiser et al., Am J Forensic Med Pathol 2001](https://doi.org/10.1097/00000433-200109000-00022)). The drug, the illness, behavior and housing are tangled together. The most defensible message for clinicians is that these prescriptions **identify** who needs heat outreach. Whether the drug itself must change is a separate and weaker claim.

## Dose adjustment and closer monitoring: dehydration turns renal drugs into accumulation risks

The "adjust or monitor" bucket centers on one chain of events. Heat and sweating cause fluid loss, which lowers kidney perfusion. Drugs that the kidneys clear then build up, and electrolytes drift. The evidence is strongest for the combination of diuretics with renin-angiotensin drugs, and for lithium and hyponatremia. For everything else in this bucket, the case is extrapolated from dehydration case reports.

An Australian study of 6,700 veterans found the highest risk of dehydration or heat-illness admission after **starting an ACE inhibitor plus diuretic combination (ratio 2.79)**. Risk was also raised after starting anticoagulants, NSAIDs, antipsychotics, antidepressants and anticholinergics. Anticonvulsants, anti-Parkinson drugs, hypnotics, anxiolytics and antihistamines were null ([Kalisch Ellett et al., J Clin Pharm Ther 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpt.12418)). Among older inpatients on thiazides or ACEi/ARBs, higher ambient temperature tracked with rising creatinine ([PLoS One 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27992525/)). Yet the "triple whammy" of diuretic plus NSAID plus ACEi/ARB **did not predict in-hospital AKI** during heat waves (OR 1.02) in South Australia ([Mangoni et al., Adv Ther 2017](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/the-concomitant-use-of-diuretics-non-steroidal-anti-inflammatory-/)). A large Ontario study confirmed heat raises AKI in older adults (aOR 1.11), but reported no drug-specific estimates ([McTavish et al., Am J Kidney Dis 2018](https://core.ac.uk/works/9186318)). Diuretics also produce counter-signals. Non-loop diuretics showed no heat-wave interaction in a 2026 Japanese study, where ACEi/ARBs and calcium-channel blockers did (on-drug IRR 1.64 vs 1.42 off, p<0.01) ([Harley et al., Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.70447)). Diuretics also did not raise core temperature in trials ([Hospers 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102886)). And a controlled experiment found **no effect of antihypertensive medication on the environmental limits people can tolerate** ([Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00038.2025)).

Hyponatremia (low blood sodium) is the best-quantified electrolyte harm. Swedish national registers show hyponatremia admissions climbing steeply above about 15°C. Admissions are **almost tenfold higher on the hottest days and about 15-fold higher in people 80 and over during heat waves** ([Swedish register study, JCEM 2022](https://dx.doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgac103)). Thiazides and SSRIs together may cause roughly 30% of hyponatremia hospitalizations ([Karolinska thesis](https://openarchive.ki.se/articles/thesis/From_drugs_to_climate_associations_between_external_factors_and_profound_hyponatremia/29064197/1/files/58427545.pdf)). Reports of drug-induced hyponatremia rise with warm-season temperature ([IJERPH 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph14070827)). This collides directly with the generic advice to "drink more," a conflict taken up in the guidance section.

Lithium is the only renally cleared drug with direct temperature-exposure data. Arizona poison-center data tie chronic lithium toxicity to ambient temperature rather than season ([University of Arizona](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/669553)). A Dutch dataset of more than 41,000 lithium levels shows they vary with temperature category ([Wilting et al., Utrecht](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/27401/wilting_07_theimpactofenvironmentaltemperature.pdf?sequence=2); journal PMID unconfirmed). A Japanese case of heat illness that unmasked lithium intoxication needed hemodialysis. It is available only as a congress abstract ([APAMT poster](https://apamt.org/assets/Uploads/Congress-Abstracts/17th-Congress/17th_Poster_Presentation_21.pdf)). For dabigatran, a fatal case was explicitly linked to summer diarrhea and low fluid intake ([PubMed 25534224](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25534224/)). For metformin, lactic acidosis follows dehydration-driven AKI ([PMC6424540](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6424540/)). For digoxin, toxicity in renal failure can rebound after antidote treatment ([Cureus](https://www.cureus.com/articles/427528-digoxin-toxicity-in-renal-failure-resolution-with-plasma-exchange-after-fab-therapy-failure.pdf)). None of these last three is heat-specific.

Diabetes drugs pull in two directions. A small 1980 sauna study showed heat speeds insulin absorption from injection sites and lowers blood glucose, so the author advised a snack or a lower dose before sauna use ([Koivisto, BMJ 1980](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1601709)). Heat effects likely differ by formulation ([PMC3230259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3230259)). For SGLT2 inhibitors, concern about volume depletion and euglycemic DKA is mechanistic. Ramadan data show modest volume-depletion signals and no reported SGLT2-inhibitor DKA ([Thieme review](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-0044-1791484.pdf)), and no study combines heat, SGLT2 inhibitors and DKA. Warfarin INR seasonality is inconsistent across studies ([PubMed 33903173](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33903173/)).

## Hold or substitute: sweat-blocking drugs produce the rarest and gravest cases

The "hold or substitute" bucket covers drugs whose heat harm can be catastrophic in an individual patient even though it is rare across the population. The best-documented example is a pair of antiseizure drugs, **topiramate and zonisamide**. Both inhibit carbonic anhydrase in sweat glands and can produce oligohidrosis (sharply reduced sweating). FDA review of zonisamide reports found **all US cases were in patients 18 or younger**, at a reporting rate about tenfold Japan's ([Knudsen et al., Pediatr Neurol 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0887-8994(02)00511-8)). Pooled estimates put the risk near **1 case per 4,600 to 10,000 patient-years**, with hyperthermia reports clustering in summer and full reversal after stopping the drug ([Low et al., Epilepsy Res 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eplepsyres.2004.06.009)). In a topiramate pilot study, 9 of 14 patients had abnormal sweat tests, but **only 3 had symptoms** ([Ben-Zeev et al., J Child Neurol 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/08830738030180041001)). That gap implies a large hidden population with impaired sweating that only decompensates under heat and exertion. In a Turkish series, 22 of 173 patients had reduced-sweating symptoms, and most of them were under 6 ([Turk J Pediatr 2012](https://turkjpediatr.org/article/download/1678/1658)).

The extreme tail reaches adults and non-epilepsy uses. A 40-year-old man on topiramate died of heat stroke (rectal temperature 43.2°C) even though **his drug levels were within the normal therapeutic range** ([Borron et al., Am J Emerg Med 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2013.07.013)). For a prescriber, this means blood-level monitoring offers no protection. Another adult was left with permanent cerebellar and cognitive damage ([Galicia et al., Clin Neuropharmacol 2005](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.wnf.0000159957.95134.1b)). A 57-year-old taking topiramate for essential tremor developed heat stroke ([Canel et al., J Med Case Rep 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-016-0835-5)), and reversible hypohidrosis has been reported on topiramate for migraine prevention ([Headache 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1526-4610.2010.01636.x)). Because topiramate is now widely prescribed by neurologists, headache specialists and for weight loss, the prescriber pool is much larger than "epilepsy clinics." By analogy, risk is often assumed for **acetazolamide**, which shares the mechanism and appears on the CDC list. No human case of acetazolamide anhidrosis was found, so its listing rests on inference.

**Anticholinergics** have a small but vivid case literature. An older man on **oxybutynin** for bladder symptoms was admitted with heat stroke on San Francisco's hottest day, and he had been admitted with a higher temperature **the previous summer**, a recurrent, seasonal harm from a urology drug rarely thought of as dangerous ([Adubofour et al., Ann Pharmacother 1996](https://doi.org/10.1177/106002809603000207)). A second case combined oxybutynin with Parkinson's-related sweating failure ([Ahmad et al., Cureus 2021](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.14701)). The most important null finding sits here too. A double-blind crossover RCT of **50 mg diphenhydramine** in 20 adults walking in 30°C heat found **no difference in sweat loss, sweating onset, rectal temperature rise or heart rate** ([Newhouse et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2024](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003527)). This contradicts guidance that singles out anticholinergic antihistamines. The study involved healthy young adults at one dose, though, and says nothing about older people on several anticholinergic drugs at once.

**Antipsychotics combined with anticholinergics** in patients doing outdoor work produce some of the starkest cases. A 36-year-old roofer on zuclopenthixol, quetiapine, benztropine, carbamazepine and levothyroxine died of heat stroke on the third day of a heat wave ([Kao & Kelly, Can J Clin Pharmacol 2007](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:18025545&resultType=core&format=json); PMID 18025545, DOI not given in notes). Another patient was admitted twice in one summer after working in a car park, **despite heat-stroke advice after the first episode** ([Kwok & Chan, Ann Pharmacother 2005](https://doi.org/10.1345/aph.1G130)). Clinicians struggle to separate heat stroke from neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) and serotonin syndrome ([Lee et al., Acta Neuropsychiatr 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/neu.2015.29)). **No study shows that ambient heat triggers NMS, serotonin syndrome or parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome.** That is a genuine evidence gap and should not be presented as "heat probably contributes."

Stimulants and antiplatelets give mixed signals. In 238,168 US Army enlistees, methylphenidate, opioids and NSAIDs raised the odds of **mild** heat illness but not severe heat illness. Risk peaked in month 2 of training ([Nelson et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2018](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001623)). Case reports include a 15-year-old football player on ADHD medication who died of exertional heat stroke ([Allen & Cross, Pediatr Emerg Care 2014](https://doi.org/10.1097/PEC.0000000000000296)). They also include a 1982 case in which one child on prescribed appetite suppressants died of heat stroke on a hike that **107 other children completed unharmed** ([Kew et al., S Afr Med J 1982](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:6128802&resultType=core&format=json); PMID 6128802). Medicare found stimulants null overall ([Layton 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0243665)). Low-dose aspirin and clopidogrel raised core temperature in heat in middle-aged adults ([Bruning et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2013](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e31827981dc)). In adults over 65, aspirin reduced skin blood flow **without** raising core temperature ([Fisher et al., Physiol Rep 2025](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70375)). Non-selective beta-blockers raised core temperature by about 0.1°C in trials ([Hospers 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102886)) but showed no effect on hospitalization in Medicare.

Antipsychotics can also cause the opposite problem. Olanzapine and other strongly 5-HT2-blocking antipsychotics have caused **severe hypothermia**, mostly in the first days after starting or raising the dose ([Kreuzer et al., J Clin Pharmacol 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0091270011409233)). Thermoregulatory drug harm runs in both directions, which matters for any graphic that implies a single arrow toward "overheating."

## Storage, devices and outages: insulin is sturdier than labeled, but emergency drugs and meters are not

The storage and device bucket overturns one common assumption and confirms several neglected ones. **Insulin is more heat-robust than its labeling implies.** In a randomized crossover trial, glargine pens kept at 37°C for 21 days gave the same continuous-glucose readings as refrigerated pens (158.7 vs 157.0 mg/dL) ([Kongmalai et al., BMJ Open Diabetes Res Care 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36585035/)). Insulins cycled between 25 and 37°C to mimic a Kenyan refugee camp kept full potency and bioactivity for 4 weeks ([Kaufmann et al., PLoS One 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33534816/)). Simulated summer mail shipping did not reduce potency either ([Chandler et al., AJHP 2008](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp070347)). An older rabbit study found potency loss at 32–37°C, so the picture is not unanimous ([Vimalavathini & Gitanjali 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19797814/)). The real insulin risk lies **above 37°C** (cars, direct sun) and in **pumps**. Bench work shows pump reservoirs can exceed 37°C during long charging or in a 35°C room, although the study was industry-sponsored and a competitor disputed its relevance ([Vereshchetin et al., Med Devices 2016](https://doi.org/10.2147/MDER.S116666)). A Swiss case report describes DKA after a pump was exposed to heat and sunlight ([Pryce, Praxis 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19424950/)). A frequently cited 1999 Diabetes Care sauna-pump DKA case could not be verified.

**Fentanyl patches are the clearest omission in heat guidance.** The US boxed warning states that heat on the patch site (heating pads, electric blankets, saunas, hot tubs, sunbathing), and also fever and exertion, increases absorption and **"has resulted in fatal overdose"** ([FDA archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090117201018/http:/www.fda.gov/cder/drug/infopage/fentanyl/default.htm)). Documented cases include an accidental sauna death ([Am J Forensic Med Pathol 2020](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/amjforensicmedicine/abstract/10.1097/paf.0000000000000565~death-in-sauna-associated-with-a-transdermal-fentanyl-patch?redirectionsource=fulltextview)). They also include a 77-year-old who sunbathed with patches and developed life-threatening coma plus full-thickness burns ([J Med Case Rep 2012](https://jmedicalcasereports.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1752-1947-6-220)). Human and lab studies show the heat effect on patch drug levels **can persist for hours after the heat source is removed**, so the danger window can extend into sleep ([FDA Butrans label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2013/021306s016lbl.pdf); [Zhang et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7644256)). Finland's regulator issued a public warning against wearing opioid patches in the sauna ([Fimea](https://fimea.fi/en/-/fimea-issues-a-warning-wearing-an-opioid-patch-in-the-sauna-may-cause-life-threatening-poisoning)). Buprenorphine, nicotine and rivastigmine patches carry similar heat cautions ([Health Canada rivastigmine monograph](https://pdf.hres.ca/dpd_pm/00075541.PDF); [PMC3175749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3175749/)). No primary heat-harm studies were found for clonidine, estradiol or scopolamine patches. The prescribers are pain medicine, palliative care, oncology and geriatrics, plus neurology for rivastigmine and rotigotine.

**Epinephrine depends on concentration and duration of exposure.** Auto-injectors held at a constant 70°C for 10 days delivered **about 78% of their labeled dose** ([Rachid et al., Drug Dev Ind Pharm 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25997362/)). A systematic review found real-world temperature swings caused no significant degradation, and all 40 devices tested still fired ([Parish et al., Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27221065/)). Dilute 0.1 mg/mL epinephrine, used in cardiac arrest, degraded substantially under repeated heating, while 1 mg/mL did not ([Grant et al., Am J Emerg Med 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8179739/)). Across EMS field studies, **lorazepam and succinylcholine** are the reliably heat-fragile drugs, while midazolam held up ([McMullan et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24548058/); [De Winter et al., Ann Emerg Med 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23711878/)). The data on ketamine conflict. South African ambulances recorded more than 30% ketamine loss within 4 weeks, but that study's control samples looked analytically suspect ([Sobuwa et al., Afr J Emerg Med 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38173689/)). A US study found no ketamine degradation even at 120°F ([Foertsch et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34028315/)). Lyophilized tenecteplase lost clot-dissolving activity after only 8 hours at about 45°C ([Henkel et al., Prehosp Disaster Med 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32686630/)). The inside of a Qatari response car reached 65.7°C ([Alaghawani et al., BMC Emerg Med 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40596924/)).

The only well-documented case of a heat-degraded drug becoming **toxic rather than weak** is historical. Degraded, outdated tetracycline caused reversible Fanconi syndrome (a kidney tubule injury) in 1960s and 1970s case reports, including from hot, humid Thailand ([PubMed 5547594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5547594/)), and animal work identified the responsible breakdown product ([Benitz & Diermeier 1964](https://www.ebm-journal.org/journals/experimental-biology-and-medicine/articles/10.3181/00379727-115-29082/pdf)). For inhalers, albuterol stored six months in a Tucson car trunk delivered normally once returned to room temperature. Delivery changed only when the inhaler was *used* outside its recommended temperature range ([University of Arizona](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/effects-of-extreme-temperatures-on-drug-delivery-of-albuterol-sul/); ID unverified).

**Glucose meters and test strips** give errors of 20–30% after brief heat and humidity exposure, and the direction of the error depends on strip chemistry ([Louie et al. 2014](https://db.cngb.org/data_resources/literature/24876542), PMID unconfirmed; [PMC2717087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717087); [PMC5032940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5032940)). Mail-order packages spent an average of **68% of transit time outside controlled room temperature** ([Chowdhury et al., JAPhA 2023](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4VZnxvW4); PMID unconfirmed). However, no study links porch or mailbox heat to clinical failure.

Power outages are where storage problems turn into systems problems. During the 2003 NYC-area blackout, EMS calls rose about 250%, and **half of respiratory calls involved failed home equipment** ([Rand et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16263671/)). Modeling projects that heat-wave mortality would **more than double** during a multiday blackout in Atlanta, Detroit and Phoenix ([Stone et al., Environ Sci Technol 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37219950/)). Florida nursing homes that lost power after Hurricane Irma had higher 7-day mortality (OR 1.25) ([Skarha et al., JAMA Health Forum 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamahealthforum.2021.3900)). Houston ED visits for dialysis and medication refills surged after Hurricane Beryl ([Caton et al., Disaster Med Public Health Prep 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40545799/)). In Victoria, Australia, heat-related ambulance risk nearly doubled during outages ([Xu et al., Environ Res 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40692025/)).

## Masked warning signs, harms from holding drugs, and overlooked populations cut across every bucket

Several harms are hard to see coming. A recurring theme is that **heat hides its own warning signs in medicated patients**. Topiramate's sweat-test abnormalities are usually silent ([Ben-Zeev 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/08830738030180041001)). Heat stroke in antipsychotic users looks like NMS or serotonin syndrome ([Lee et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/neu.2015.29)). Fever on clozapine can be benign or the first sign of something serious ([Verdoux et al., Schizophr Res 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.schres.2019.07.040)). And fentanyl-related sedation from heat or fever can be mistaken for heat illness ([Americares provider guide](https://www.americares.org/wp-content/uploads/HEAT_Medications_ForProviders_FINAL0904.pdf)). Wearables add a new blind spot. Core-temperature estimators based on heart rate have been validated only in soldiers, athletes and firefighters, and they **miss many readings above 39°C** even in those groups ([Front Sports Act Living 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.882254/pdf); [MIT Lincoln Lab](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1034034.pdf)). No validation exists in people on beta-blockers, which blunt heart rate, or on stimulants and anticholinergics, which raise it. Those are exactly the patients a heat-alert app would target. Glucose strips can misread in heat, so advice to check glucose more often can generate unreliable numbers. That last point is an inference, not a documented harm.

**Holding a drug can itself cause harm**, which is why guidance universally says "do not stop abruptly." Abruptly stopping antiparkinsonian drugs causes parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome. Across 56 published cases, it mainly affects elderly patients with long-standing disease and carries real mortality. A related syndrome, dyskinesia-hyperpyrexia, comes from too much drug instead ([Wang et al., J Parkinsons Dis 2022](https://doi.org/10.3233/JPD-223362)). Battery failure of a deep brain stimulator can trigger a similar crisis ([BMJ Case Rep 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2018-229122)), as can missed levodopa doses during fasting or nil-by-mouth periods ([Nutr Clin Pract 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ncp.11297)). For heart failure, no source addresses the risk of decompensation when diuretics are cut back in heat. That leaves cardiologists with "review and adjust" advice and no titration guidance.

Special populations sharpen the picture. In pregnancy, a secondary analysis of a large aspirin RCT found that low-dose aspirin appeared to **blunt the rise in preterm birth with humid heat**, but heat was still linked to perinatal mortality among aspirin users ([BIDMC press release on JAMA Netw Open 2026](https://bidmc.org/news-stories/all-news-stories/news/2026/05/bidmc-study-aspirin-preterm-births-heat-risk)). An Israeli cohort of 131,599 births found no significant medication interactions ([HUJI](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/ambient-temperature-and-preterm-birth-interaction-by-maternal-med/)). In dialysis patients, extreme heat raised same-day admission (RR 1.27) and death (RR 1.31), with no drug-stratified analysis ([JAMA Netw Open 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6692691)). Multiple sclerosis offers a rare protective signal. Small crossover trials suggest 4-aminopyridine (dalfampridine) may widen heat tolerance ([IJESAB](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol2/iss8/2)). In Texas prisons, more than 250 deaths were attributed to heat, with no heat-mortality link in air-conditioned facilities ([Grist on Skarha et al. 2022](https://grist.org/equity/texas-prisons-heat-air-conditioning-study/)), and authors cite psychotropic use as a vulnerability ([Skarha et al., PLoS One](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Heat-related-mortality-in-U.S.-state-and-private-prisons.pdf)). In workers, **73% of fatal heat illnesses in OSHA investigations occurred in the first week on the job**, and many involved predisposing medications ([Tustin et al., J Occup Environ Med 2018](https://doi.org/10.1097/JOM.0000000000001365)). That points to the first weeks of heat exposure, not the whole season, as the window when drug risk matters most.

Sun-related drug harms are the best-established skin category. Long-term **voriconazole** in lung-transplant patients raises squamous-cell skin cancer risk (meta-analysis RR 1.72) ([JAAD 2019 via curehunter](https://www.curehunter.com/public/pubmed30130598.do); [Singer 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371090)). **Hydrochlorothiazide** carries a dose-dependent non-melanoma skin-cancer risk that prompted a UK regulatory update ([Pedersen et al., JAAD 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29217346/); [MHRA](https://gov.uk/drug-safety-update/hydrochlorothiazide-risk-of-non-melanoma-skin-cancer-particularly-in-long-term-use)). Hydrochlorothiazide is therefore a single drug appearing in both the dehydration and sun-exposure columns of a heat map.

## Guidance converges on "review, don't stop" but omits patches, SGLT2 inhibitors and meter errors

All the major documents agree on one principle: **no blanket stopping of medications**. Changes should be individualized after a risk-benefit review ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/hcp/clinical-guidance/heat-and-medications-guidance-for-clinicians.html); [GOV.UK UKHSA](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/supporting-vulnerable-people-before-and-during-hot-weather-healthcare-professionals)). Beyond that, the documents differ widely in how specific they are and in what they cover.

The guidance falls short in four ways. **First, it misses exposure-route and device harms.** The fentanyl heat warning sits in the product label, and Finland's regulator has publicized it, but it has not migrated into any heat-health document reviewed. Pump overheating and meter errors are similarly absent outside France. **Second, the drug lists predate newer classes.** SGLT2 inhibitors, now prescribed across endocrinology, cardiology and nephrology, appear in no official document; only a commercial patient blog groups them with diuretics ([Pillo](https://pillo.care/blog/medications-and-heat-risk)). Health Canada itself warned in 2011 that understudied drugs may be missing. **Third, the documents give contradictory hydration advice.** CDC and NYC advise relaxing fluid restrictions on hot days, while Health Canada warns that overdrinking causes hyponatremia, a real risk for thiazide and SSRI users given the Swedish data. Health Canada lists "hyperhydration, hyponatremia" for diuretics, while CDC, NYC and UKHSA list dehydration. CDC's own table says SSRIs increase sweating while its narrative says they impair it. **Fourth, the evidence behind the lists is opaque.** Only Health Canada states odds ratios or flags non-significant ones. None acknowledges the null within-person studies, the diphenhydramine RCT, or the finding that insulin is robust at 37°C. Guidance therefore overstates certainty for common drug classes, where population evidence shows modest and possibly confounded effects. At the same time it understates rare, severe harms that are documented in case reports and labels: topiramate in adults, oxybutynin, fentanyl patches and pump insulin. No peer-reviewed comparison of medication content across heat action plans was found. A 2026 scoping review concluded that "specific, evidence-based guidance on managing drug-heat interactions is limited" ([Tews et al., Pharmacy 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/pharmacy14030074)).

## True evidence gaps and how to map them visually

Several gaps appear to be genuine absences in the literature rather than artifacts of limited searching, though none can be confirmed as absolute. No study tests whether **night-time heat or humid heat** (wet-bulb or heat index) modifies medication risk. None estimates how medication use shifts the temperature at which mortality is lowest. No sex- or age-stratified drug-by-heat estimates were found. **No study shows heat triggers NMS, serotonin syndrome or parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome**, and no seasonal NMS analysis exists. There is no population-level heat evidence for opioids, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, PPIs, levothyroxine, cholinesterase inhibitors, antiplatelets, or for lithium specifically. **No human case of acetazolamide, glycopyrrolate or botulinum-toxin heat harm** was found despite plausible mechanisms. No heat-absorption data exist for GLP-1 agonists or heparin, and no heat-stability data for GLP-1 pens, glucagon kits, levothyroxine, ophthalmic drops, monoclonal antibodies or vaccines. Wearable heat-strain estimators have **never been validated in medicated, older, dialysis, MS or pregnant users**. No drug-stratified analyses exist for dialysis, transplant (tacrolimus), HIV, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, spinal cord injury or heart failure. Also missing are outcome studies linking mail-order heat exposure to clinical failure, measured medication or device failures in the 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, heart-failure outcomes from heat-season diuretic adjustment, and studies of methadone or buprenorphine patients in heat waves. Several frequently repeated claims could not be traced to primary sources: potassium supplementation for furosemide users, a 2017 UK diabetes case-crossover null result, a 1999 sauna-pump DKA case, and a fever-only fentanyl case. They should appear on a visual only with an "unverified" marker.

The findings translate into a visual in a few concrete ways. The core display is an **evidence-versus-guidance quadrant**. The x-axis is evidence strength, from the six tiers above. The y-axis is guidance coverage, counted as the number of the eight jurisdictions that address the harm with a specific action. Four clusters emerge. **High evidence, high guidance** holds antipsychotics, anticholinergics, and diuretics with ACEi/ARB, with a warning badge for confounding. **Low evidence, high guidance** holds diphenhydramine and other antihistamines, SSRIs and acetazolamide. **High-severity but case-level evidence with low guidance** holds fentanyl patches, topiramate in adults, oxybutynin and pump insulin. **Mechanism only with no guidance** holds SGLT2 inhibitors. Encoding severity as marker size keeps rare fatal harms visible next to common modest ones. A second panel can map the four action buckets against prescriber specialty, showing how many specialties each bucket touches and that neurology, urology and pain medicine own several of the most severe tail harms. Null findings and counter-signals (diphenhydramine RCT, Japan SCCS, Mangoni AKI, insulin at 37°C, non-loop diuretics) deserve their own visual treatment, such as hollow markers. That way the map shows where evidence argues *against* intervention as well as for it. A third layer can flag population mismatch by shading items whose controlled evidence comes only from healthy young men. A final strip can show "harm from holding" (parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome, heart-failure decompensation) next to each "hold or substitute" recommendation, so counseling planners see both sides of the trade-off.

## Conclusion

The evidence suggests that heat-wave medication counseling has been aimed partly at the wrong question. The common classes on every list mainly tell clinicians **who** to call before a heat wave: older patients on antipsychotics, anticholinergics, or diuretics with RAAS drugs, especially those without air conditioning, in institutions, or in their first week of outdoor work. Whether to change the drug is a separate and weakly supported decision, and abrupt changes carry their own documented harms. The actionable, drug-specific advice that does have support lives in places guidance rarely looks. It means warning patients away from heat sources while wearing fentanyl and other patches, and screening children and adults on topiramate or zonisamide for reduced sweating. It also means treating oxybutynin and similar bladder drugs as heat risks, protecting pumps and glucose strips, tailoring fluid advice for patients on thiazides and SSRIs, and planning for power outages that turn storage problems into emergencies.

For health systems, the practical implication is that a heat plan works less like a drug list and more like a set of checks. Outreach should be triggered by prescription data, and advice should depend on the route of exposure (patch, pump, injectable). Device-failure planning should be tied to the grid, and fluid advice should distinguish hyponatremia-prone patients from fluid-restricted ones. The biggest research leverage would come from drug-stratified studies in the populations that actually die in heat, and from validating wearables in medicated patients. Both are cheap compared with continuing to build guidance on 2003-era mechanism lists and on laboratory trials in healthy young men.
