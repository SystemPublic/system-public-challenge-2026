01 — Transmission

It begins
with a bite.

A single infected mosquito can introduce Plasmodium parasites into the human body. What happens next unfolds far beyond the surface of the skin.

02 — Into the body

An invisible
passenger.

Infectious sporozoites move from the bite site into circulation, taking a brief journey toward the liver.

SPOROZOITE STAGE
03 — Liver stage

A quiet
takeover.

Inside liver cells, malaria parasites replicate before the first symptoms appear. One infection can give rise to thousands of new parasites.

ASYMPTOMATIC DEVELOPMENT
04 — Blood stage

The cycle
accelerates.

New parasites invade red blood cells, reproduce, and rupture them. Repeating cycles of infection cause many of malaria’s symptoms.

ERYTHROCYTIC CYCLE
05 — Treatment

Medicine can
interrupt it.

Antimalarial medicines disrupt parasite growth. Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine, known as SP, is used as a preventive treatment against P. falciparum malaria.

DIFFERENT DRUGS · DIFFERENT TARGETS
06 — Resistance

But evolution
doesn't stop.

Under drug pressure, certain genetic variants can become more common. For SP, the resistance markers sit in the parasite’s folate pathway genes, dhfr and dhps. Their significance depends on the drug, parasite background, and supporting evidence.

EVOLUTION, NOT INEVITABILITY
07 — Evidence surveillance

A mutation
is a clue.

A resistance marker is a signal, not proof of treatment failure. When new evidence changes the picture, targeted alerts help scientists verify the finding, investigate its context, and decide what needs closer surveillance.

DETECT · VERIFY · INFORM THE RESPONSE
08 — The mission

Understand the biology. Explore historical observations. Detect when new research changes the picture for a resistance marker.
